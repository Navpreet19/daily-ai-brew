## Editor's Open

Tuesday, and the AI trade just learned that "winning" and "going up" aren't the same thing anymore. Memory-chip stocks — the ones nobody outside finance Twitter could name six months ago — cratered into a bear market almost overnight, even as TSMC quietly posted the best quarter in its history. Meanwhile Brussels told Google and Apple to open their phones to the competition, and Alibaba shipped a model that claims to be the second-smartest thing on Earth like it was no big deal. Grab your coffee — today's issue has more plot than usual.

## The Lead Stories

### The Chips Are Down (Just Not All of Them)

Memory-chip stocks just did what AI compute stocks haven't: they cracked. Micron, Samsung, SK Hynix, and the Roundhill Memory ETF have each fallen more than 20% from recent highs — the technical definition of a bear market — as roughly $3.3 trillion has evaporated from global chip stocks since June 22, with Micron alone down about $350 billion. Sandisk, Intel, Applied Materials, and Lam Research have each shed over $100 billion too. The trigger: Moonshot's Kimi K3 reignited "DeepSeek moment" fears that China can match US AI on the cheap, layered onto existing jitters about dot-com-era valuations and a hawkish Fed. Nvidia, notably, is barely down. And two days later, TSMC posted a record $40.2 billion quarter, with AI-chip-driven revenue up 20% in three months — so demand for actual compute hasn't blinked.

**Bottom line:** This isn't the AI bubble popping — it's the market finally pricing memory and compute as two very different bets.

### Brussels Redraws Your Home Screen

The European Commission issued binding Digital Markets Act orders this week forcing Google to give rival AI assistants the same access to Android that Gemini enjoys — voice activation, in-app actions like booking a taxi, the works — by July 2027, and to start sharing anonymized search data with competing AI chatbots from January. Google's Android chief called the ruling "the wrong track," warning it hands "sensitive and powerful device permissions" to outside apps. Apple, caught in the same regulatory current, went further: it says it won't launch its new Siri AI assistant in the EU at all, arguing Brussels' rules would force it to give any third-party AI "nearly unlimited access to a user's device" with the ability to act autonomously. Both companies are framing this as a privacy fight; the EU is framing it as ending a monopoly on your phone's front door.

**Bottom line:** The EU just decided your AI assistant shouldn't be whichever one your phone maker picked for you — Big Tech's counter is that the alternative might be less safe.

### China Ships a Second Frontier Model Before Lunch

Days after Moonshot's Kimi K3 grabbed headlines as the largest open-weight model ever released, Alibaba previewed Qwen3.8-Max at the World AI Conference in Shanghai — a 2.4-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts model and the first Qwen system above a trillion parameters to handle text, images, video, and documents together. Alibaba's own benchmarks (not yet independently verified) put it second only to Anthropic's Claude Fable 5 among the systems it tested. It's live now through Alibaba's Token Plan and Qoder platforms at a preview discount — 10% of standard pricing — with a full open-weight release promised soon, though the benchmark table, model card, and license are still TBD.

**Bottom line:** Export controls were supposed to slow this down. Instead, Chinese labs are shipping frontier models back-to-back like a product roadmap, not a moonshot.

### The Entry-Level Job Market Has a New Villain

The numbers on AI and jobs are getting harder to wave away. The US has logged 302 AI-tagged layoff events so far in 2026, touching roughly 201,754 workers — about 1,000 job losses a day on average — with 54% of this year's layoff announcements explicitly citing AI, automation, or machine learning. The pain isn't evenly spread: payroll declines are concentrated in financial-services and information-sector jobs, down roughly 28,000 a month, while Goldman Sachs estimates AI has trimmed monthly payroll growth by about 16,000 jobs nationally — mostly by companies hiring fewer junior staff, not firing existing ones outright. Stanford's Digital Economy Lab backs that up: employment is softening specifically in roles AI can automate, while holding steady in roles where AI just makes people faster.

**Bottom line:** This isn't a mass-layoff story, it's a "no one's hiring the entry-level version of your job" story — arguably scarier if you're still in school.

## The Big Picture

Zoom out and today's stories are all the same story: the AI industry is splitting into winners and losers within categories that used to move together. Chip stocks used to rise as one trade — now compute (TSMC, Nvidia) and memory (Micron, Samsung, SK Hynix) are telling opposite stories in the same week. The labor market used to fear "AI takes jobs" as one monolith — now it's specifically entry-level, automatable roles thinning out while AI-augmented roles hold steady. Even geopolitics is bifurcating: the EU is legislating interoperability while Chinese labs ship two frontier-scale models in a single week like it's a routine product cadence, not a moonshot. The lesson for anyone building, investing in, or just trying to survive this cycle: stop asking "is AI good or bad for X" and start asking "which specific part of X."
