## Editor's Open

Welcome to the second half of 2026, where the AI race has quietly changed events. The first half was about who could build the smartest model. The scoreboard now tracks who can run one cheapest, serve it fastest, and keep the lights on underneath it. Today's slate has all three — plus a squid-shaped reminder that the machines are now debugging us. Let's get into it.

## The Lead Stories

### Sonnet Boom

Anthropic launched Claude Sonnet 5 on June 30 and made it the default for every Free and Pro user worldwide as of July 1. The pitch, per TechCrunch: "a cheaper way to run agents." At introductory pricing of $2 per million input tokens and $10 output (through August 31), it costs less than the Sonnet 4.6 it replaces while landing close to flagship Opus 4.8 on Anthropic's published benchmarks — including a 20-point jump on terminal-based agent tasks. The timing isn't subtle: enterprises spent Q2 gasping at agentic AI bills, and Anthropic is racing toward a reported October IPO. One catch for builders — it's not a drop-in swap. Sampling parameters are gone, extended thinking is on by default, and a new tokenizer means the same text can cost up to 35% more tokens.

**Bottom line:** The frontier is now a pricing page. When near-flagship capability ships at below last-gen prices, the moat stops being intelligence and starts being unit economics.

### Seoul Power

South Korea's monthly exports crossed $100 billion for the first time ever in June — $102.25 billion, up 70.9% year over year — and the engine was almost entirely AI, per UPI. Semiconductor shipments hit a record $44.82 billion, nearly tripling from a year earlier, as US and Chinese hyperscalers scrambled for high-bandwidth memory and DDR5 to feed AI servers. That's one country exporting more chips in a month than many countries export of everything. It lands days after Seoul announced a $1 trillion-plus AI and chip investment drive (Al Jazeera), a bid to make sure the memory boom doesn't stay a memory.

**Bottom line:** The AI buildout's profits aren't just flowing to Santa Clara. Korea's memory makers are the quiet arms dealers of the compute war — and their government just went all-in on keeping it that way.

### Watt's the Big Idea

Britain's National Grid is putting $1.75 billion into Joulent, a US energy platform building power infrastructure for AI data centers, taking a 35% stake (PRNewswire). First up: Project Kilby, a 2.67-gigawatt gas-fired plant in West Texas — a 50/50 venture with Chevron — feeding a Microsoft-operated data center under a 20-year power deal, targeting first power by 2028. Translation: a 200-year-old British utility just bought a ticket to the American AI gold rush, because the grid interconnection queue is now the industry's longest line. Data Center Knowledge notes the deal was spurred precisely by those interconnect delays — if you can't plug in, you build your own plug.

**Bottom line:** Compute follows power, and power is the new scarce asset. The smart infrastructure money has stopped asking "which model wins" and started asking "who owns the electrons."

### Squid Pro Quo

Meet Squidbleed (CVE-2026-47729): a memory-leak vulnerability in the Squid proxy server that quietly exposed HTTP credentials for 29 years — surviving nearly three decades of human code reviews and audits — until Claude Mythos 5 found it through Anthropic's Project Glasswing research program, per the disclosure covered by Build Fast with AI. Glasswing partners report 23,019 AI-flagged vulnerabilities across 1,000 open-source projects in the program's first month, with a claimed 90.6% confirmation rate. The irony writes itself: the same class of model governments restricted over cybersecurity fears just found a bug every human security team on Earth missed. If you run Squid, patch now and assume proxied HTTP credentials were exposed.

**Bottom line:** AI-powered code auditing just went from demo to public service. The uncomfortable corollary: whatever defenders can find at this scale, attackers can too — patch faster.

## The Big Picture

Six months ago the AI story was capability: who's smartest. Today's issue barely mentions benchmarks, and that's the tell. Sonnet 5 competes on price. Cerebras competes on speed. Korea competes on supply. National Grid competes on electrons. The frontier hasn't stopped advancing — it's just that intelligence is becoming table stakes, and the margin is moving to whoever runs it cheapest, serves it fastest, and powers it most reliably. That's what maturing industries look like. It's also exactly what you'd expect three months before the two biggest AI labs try to go public: nothing focuses a pricing page like an S-1.
