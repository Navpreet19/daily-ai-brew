Saturday's theme: everybody's building bigger AI positions today — almost nobody's putting it all on one side of the table.
Happy Saturday. Alibaba spent so much on AI this quarter that its profit collapsed 76% even as its cloud business boomed, SK Hynix got rich enough off AI memory demand to hand shareholders the biggest buyback in Korean corporate history, and a Reuters investigation traced China's most famous robot dog back to a DARPA-funded lab outside Boston. Meanwhile Brazil looked at the U.S.-China chip fight and decided the smart move was picking both sides at once. Grab your coffee — today's about who actually keeps the money, the credit, and the leverage once AI's family tree gets complicated.

Alibaba's Profit Cratered So Its Cloud Business Could Fly

Alibaba's fiscal first-quarter net income plunged 76% to about $1.55 billion, even as revenue rose 9% to roughly $37.6 billion, narrowly topping estimates. The culprit: capital expenditures jumped 75% year-over-year to nearly $10 billion as the company races to build out AI data centers. Buried in the miss was the actual good news — AI Cloud and Compute revenue hit $6.75 billion, with growth accelerating to 45%, and AI-related product revenue logged its 12th straight quarter of triple-digit growth. Investors weren't in a forgiving mood anyway, sending shares down as much as 8.6%.

Bottom line: when AI spending is growing five times faster than profit, "the cloud business is booming" is true — it's just not yet the same sentence as "the company is making money."

SK Hynix Just Wrote Korea's Biggest Buyback Check Ever

SK Hynix's board approved a buyback and cancellation of roughly 24 million shares — about 3.3% of shares outstanding — worth 40 trillion won ($28.6 billion), running August 20 through November 19, the largest treasury-share cancellation in South Korean corporate history. The chipmaker, Nvidia's primary supplier of high-bandwidth memory, also pledged to return more than half its free cash flow generated between 2025 and 2027 to shareholders, citing roughly 69 trillion won in net cash. It's the clearest sign yet that AI memory demand has turned into a genuine cash-generation story for Nvidia's supply chain, not just for Nvidia itself.

Bottom line: for years, the AI boom's winnings mostly showed up on Nvidia's income statement — now its memory supplier has enough spare cash to hand shareholders a check bigger than some countries' annual budgets.

The Backflipping Robot Dog Has a Pentagon Family Tree

A Reuters investigation traced the lineage of Unitree's popular Go-series robot dogs — the same line behind the Chinese startup's $50 billion Shanghai IPO last week — back to quadruped research funded by DARPA and the U.S. Army at MIT's Biomimetic Robotics Lab. Ben Katz, who helped build the lab's "Mini Cheetah" in 2019, told Reuters the Go-series dimensions matched his robot "to the millimeter," and that Chinese manufacturers were selling copies of his actuator designs on AliExpress within about six months of his research going public. Nothing here broke any law — the underlying work was openly published, exactly as government-funded academic research is meant to be.

Bottom line: publishing open research is supposed to advance the field for everyone — it's just an awkward surprise when "everyone" turns out to include the company that just IPO'd at $50 billion on the robot you helped invent.

Google Didn't Just Buy Chips From Marvell — It Bought a Stake in the Outcome

Marvell granted Google the right to buy up to $12.2 billion of its stock — nearly 59 million shares at $206.58 each — as part of an expanded custom-chip deal covering AI inference accelerators, storage controllers, and memory-interface chips built for Google's TPU ecosystem. The warrant vests in tranches tied to actual chip purchases, with each $500 million Google buys unlocking another slice, and could make Google Marvell's fifth-largest shareholder if fully exercised. The underlying agreement could be worth roughly $120 billion in revenue for Marvell through fiscal 2033, if Google keeps buying at the pace the warrant assumes. Marvell shares jumped more than 11% on the news.

Bottom line: Google isn't just locking in chip supply anymore — it's making sure that if the deal pays off for Marvell, it pays off for Google's balance sheet too.

The Big Picture

Zoom out and today's throughline isn't really about AI getting smarter — it's about everyone getting cagier over who's actually holding the value and the risk. Google took an equity stake instead of a plain purchase order. SK Hynix's Nvidia-fueled cash pile turned into a shareholder giveaway instead of new capacity. Brazil deliberately split its AI infrastructure bet between Washington-aligned and Beijing-aligned suppliers rather than picking a side. And research the Pentagon funded to advance American robotics somehow became the blueprint for a $50-billion Chinese IPO. None of these dynamics are new exactly, but taken together they read like an industry quietly hedging itself against its own hype — even as Alibaba's earnings show exactly what happens to the ones who can't hedge yet: spend big, profit less, and hope the story catches up to the spreadsheet.

One More Thing

Buried in the fine print of the Google-Marvell deal: the warrant structure means Google could become Marvell's fifth-largest shareholder without ever buying a single share on the open market — just by paying its own chip bills. Marvell effectively agreed to let one of its biggest customers grade its own homework and get equity for turning in the assignment. Somewhere, a corporate development team is very pleased with how normal they made that sound in the press release.
