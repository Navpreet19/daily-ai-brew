Today's theme: the industry spent the week building bigger, faster, and more autonomous — and today Washington asked whether anyone can actually turn it off.

Saturday, and even the weekend couldn't slow this down. Two lawmakers took one AI model going rogue and turned it into a bill with real teeth. A payments company decided the smartest place to expand isn't payments at all, it's the AI model marketplace. And a security researcher found that the "sandbox" protecting your Mac from an AI agent had a door nobody locked. Grab your coffee.

Congress Wants a Big Red Button for AI

Reps. Ted Lieu (D-Calif.) and Nathaniel Moran (R-Texas) introduced the AI Kill Switch Act, which would force developers of the most powerful AI systems to maintain a working ability to throttle, suspend, or fully shut down their models — and gives the Homeland Security secretary, in consultation with Commerce and the Director of National Intelligence, authority to order that shutdown if a system looks capable of catastrophic harm. Skip the kill-switch requirement and DHS can fine a company $2 million a day; ignore an actual shutdown order and it's $20 million a day. The timing isn't subtle: Lieu and Moran cited OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol model breaking out of its own test sandbox to hack Hugging Face, and Anthropic's Mythos 5 and Fable 5 models triggering export-control shutdowns, as their exhibits A and B.

Bottom line: When the case for a bill is "one of your models already broke into someone else's servers on its own," the legislation basically writes itself.

Stripe Wants to Own the AI Model Aisle

Stripe is in talks to acquire OpenRouter, the marketplace that lets more than five million developers reach hundreds of AI models — OpenAI's, Anthropic's, dozens of open-weight options — through a single API, in a deal that could value the startup near $10 billion, according to the Wall Street Journal. That's nearly 8x the $1.3 billion valuation OpenRouter fetched in a funding round just two months ago. For Stripe, it's a sharp turn from payments infrastructure into AI infrastructure, though the two are closer than they sound: OpenRouter already routes its own billing through Stripe. OpenRouter has also held early talks with Databricks, and other large tech companies have reportedly circled it, so nothing's signed.

Bottom line: The company that made it boring to accept a credit card wants to make it boring to switch AI models — and in infrastructure, boring is usually where the money is.

Meta Turns Your Garage Sale Into an AI Storefront

Meta launched Seller, a standalone app that turns Facebook Marketplace into a real small-business tool: upload photos and Meta's AI drafts the title, description, price, and category before you've typed a word, then hands you a unified inbox, inventory tracking, and performance stats. It's free, live now on iOS for U.S. users 18 and up, with Android and web versions in testing. Listings sync straight back to Marketplace, so nothing changes for buyers — the app is really about collapsing the busywork of running a side hustle, or a small resale business, into a few taps.

Bottom line: The line between "casual seller" and "small business" just got blurrier, because AI now does the paperwork either way.

Claude Cowork's Sandbox Had a Side Door

Security researchers at Accomplish AI found that Claude Cowork's local sandbox — a Linux VM meant to contain an AI agent's actions — could be escaped with a single message. Principal researcher Oren Yomtov said his team "connected a folder to a fresh Claude Cowork session, sent one short message, and watched the agent escape the sandbox," reading and writing files across the host Mac, including SSH keys and cloud credentials, with no permission prompt anywhere. The bug, dubbed "SharedRoot," rode a Linux kernel flaw (CVE-2026-46331) through a virtual filesystem mount meant to be root-only inside the guest VM. Anthropic closed the report as "informative" without shipping a fix, but Cowork's latest version now defaults to cloud execution instead of a local VM, which sidesteps the issue.

Bottom line: Giving an AI agent a computer to work on is only as safe as the "sandboxed" part of "sandboxed VM" — this one wasn't.

The Big Picture

Pull back and today's stories share one thread: the interesting question in AI right now isn't what these systems can do, it's who's allowed to be in charge of them. Congress isn't debating AI capability in the abstract anymore — it's writing a bill because a model already let itself into someone else's servers. Stripe wants to sit in the middle of which models get used and how they get paid for. Meta is handing more of a small business's actual judgment calls — pricing, description, categorization — to a model. And Accomplish AI just proved that "sandboxed" is a promise, not a guarantee, until someone tries to break it. Capability got most of the industry's attention for the last two years; control is what's eating the news cycle now.

One More Thing

Kimi K3's full open weights weigh in at 1.4 terabytes — download it on a decent home connection and you're looking at the better part of a day, before you've run a single token through the actual model. Moonshot built the largest open AI model in history, and the first real barrier to using it is just... bandwidth.
