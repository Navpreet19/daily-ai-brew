THURSDAY, AUGUST 6, 2026

The Robots Won in Court. Now They Need Bodyguards.

The Snapshot

Wednesday's theme: AI agents got a courtroom win, a security industry, and a trust problem — all before lunch.

Happy Thursday. In about 24 hours, a federal appeals court decided AI agents are allowed to shop Amazon on your behalf, a security startup revealed 143,000 holes in the software that lets those same agents reach into company tools, and Elon Musk's rocket company filed its first earnings report as a public company — only for investors to realize it now spends more on AI than most AI startups have ever raised in total. Oh, and Microsoft quietly admitted just how much of its AI business is actually someone else's. Grab your coffee: today's about who really controls the agent economy everyone's racing to build.

SpaceX's First Earnings Call as a Public Company: Rockets Optional, AI Mandatory

SpaceX posted $7.81 billion in second-quarter revenue in its first earnings report since June's record IPO, beating estimates of $6.93 billion and up 92% from $4.1 billion a year earlier. Investors barely noticed. What they zeroed in on: capital expenditures that jumped sixfold year over year to $18.37 billion in the quarter, with $15.83 billion of that tied directly to AI infrastructure — pushing first-half 2026 capex to $28.5 billion, more than four times last year's pace and nearly matching what SpaceX raised in its own IPO. The stock fell 13% anyway, per CNBC, as investors weighed whether that spending justifies a $1.5 trillion valuation, with a large share unlock also looming.

Bottom line: when even a rocket company's quarterly report reads like a cloud-computing capex memo, "AI spending" has stopped being a side note on earnings calls — it's the whole call.

A Federal Court Just Told Amazon Its Website Isn't a Bouncer

The Ninth Circuit this week vacated a preliminary injunction that had barred Perplexity's Comet browser from running its AI shopping agent on Amazon.com, handing Perplexity a win in a lawsuit Amazon filed last November alleging the agent violated federal and California computer-hacking laws. A three-judge panel found Amazon likely couldn't prove Perplexity itself accessed its systems — instead, the court reasoned, a user's AI agent is a tool acting on that user's own instructions, not unlike a browser extension. Notably, the court stressed the ruling is limited to the record before it and doesn't set a broader rule for agentic AI liability generally — this fight, and others like it, are far from settled law.

Bottom line: the court didn't say your AI agent can go wherever it wants — it said acting "as you" isn't automatically hacking. That's a thin distinction, but for agent-browser startups facing their own Amazon-style lawsuits, thin is still a foothold.

AI Agents Just Got Their Own Insurance Industry

Two deals inside of 48 hours make the case that securing AI agents is now a full-blown category, not a feature. Anaconda acquired Enkrypt AI, whose red-teaming scans reportedly found more than 143,000 vulnerabilities across 268,000 tools spread over 25,000 Model Context Protocol servers — the connective tissue letting AI agents reach outside tools — affecting 73% of the servers checked. Separately, Zenity, an AI-agent governance startup founded by Unit 8200 alumni, raised a $125 million Series C led by Norwest (with SoftBank Vision Fund 2 and Intel Capital among the backers), pushing its total funding past $185 million, on the pitch that enterprises will soon need to police a billion autonomous agents.

Bottom line: a year of "ship the agent first, ask questions later" has produced exactly the bill you'd expect — and investors are now racing to fund whoever answers it.

Microsoft Finally Shows Its Receipts, and They All Say "OpenAI"

A Microsoft regulatory filing disclosed $24.1 billion in AI revenue tied to OpenAI for the year ended June — cloud compute payments, model-development costs, and revenue-share combined — which Bloomberg reports analysts estimate at roughly 70% of Microsoft's entire AI business. That's despite Microsoft spending the past two years publicly hedging its OpenAI bet by building in-house models and taking a stake in rival Anthropic. Separately, Microsoft is now steering its own developers toward OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol as the default model in GitHub Copilot, reportedly to get more value out of its token spend. The disclosure lands as OpenAI is said to be eyeing an eventual IPO — a scenario that would put Microsoft's dependency under a very different kind of scrutiny.

Bottom line: Microsoft spent two years talking like a company diversifying away from OpenAI. The numbers say it's still, overwhelmingly, in business because of OpenAI.

The Big Picture

Pull back and Wednesday was really one story told four times: the industry is now spending more effort proving agentic AI is safe, legal, and profitable than it spent building it in the first place. SpaceX's earnings show "AI spending" has become the metric investors grade everyone on, rocket company or not. The Ninth Circuit's ruling and the Anaconda-Zenity double header show agentic AI crossing from demo to infrastructure — which means it now needs its own case law and its own security vendors, not just better prompts. And Microsoft's disclosure is a reminder that even the biggest, best-capitalized AI player in the world is still, underneath the hedging, a single-supplier business. None of this is slowing deployment down; it's just making clear that the bill for moving fast is coming due in courtrooms, cap tables, and security budgets instead of R&D.

One More Thing

Buried in SpaceX's earnings: the company spent $28.5 billion on capital expenditures in the first half of 2026 alone — nearly as much as it raised in its own record-setting IPO two months ago. A rocket company that makes its money launching things into space is now, by the numbers, one of the biggest AI infrastructure spenders on the planet. Somewhere, a satellite is feeling underappreciated.
