**Good morning.** It's Saturday, the one day the AI industry is contractually obligated to rest, and even it couldn't sit still. This week the genre was "be careful what you build": a model aced the government's hardest hacking test and got grounded for it, OpenAI launched its smartest systems straight into a velvet rope, and the people who actually defend networks started a petition to get the good stuff back. The throughline isn't a product. It's a question nobody's answered yet: when an AI is good enough to break into the nation's secrets, who's allowed to press "go"?

---

## Frontier, meet the velvet rope

OpenAI announced three new models on Friday — GPT-5.6 Sol, Terra, and Luna, tuned for heavy reasoning, everyday tasks, and cheap bulk jobs — and then did something it's never done: kept them behind a gate, at the U.S. government's request. Per CNBC and TechCrunch, the White House's cyber and science-policy offices asked OpenAI to limit the launch to a "small group of trusted partners" while officials build a framework for security-testing frontier models. It's the first time Washington has *preemptively* asked an American lab to throttle a release before it shipped.

The worry, per the reporting, is cyber capability — these models are good enough at finding and exploiting software flaws to make both Wall Street and Washington nervous. OpenAI complied but pushed back in the same breath, saying it doesn't believe "this kind of government access process should become the long-term default," and promising broader access through ChatGPT, Codex, and its API soon.

**Bottom line:** The era of "ship it to everyone on launch day" just hit its first government-issued speed bump. Capability and access used to be the same decision. As of Friday, they're not.

## One off switch, everybody loses

Exhibit B for the same trend, gloves off. Anthropic's most capable models — codenamed Mythos and Fable — have been effectively offline since June 12, after the administration restricted them to U.S. citizens (with no real way to verify nationality in real time, that's an off switch for everyone). The trigger: at a Senate hearing, lawmakers relayed that Mythos, tested against real classified systems, "broke into almost all of them, not in weeks, but in hours." Officials later clarified it found the holes fast but didn't necessarily walk through them.

This week, more than 100 cybersecurity experts signed a letter calling the shutdown a mistake, per Cybersecurity Dive. Their argument: these are exactly the tools defenders use to find bugs before attackers do, they're not uniquely dangerous next to what's already out there, and benching them "risked America's AI leadership without any real risk to justify it" — especially with China's best models "only months behind."

**Bottom line:** A model that can surface a 27-year-old security hole is a gift to whoever patches it and whoever attacks it — same tool, no setting that makes it only the good kind. Washington chose "nobody." The defenders are the ones who'll feel it first.

## Brain drain, now with an express lane

The talent war has a body count this week, and it's all wearing Google badges. Within days, DeepMind lost AI-coding lead Jonas Adler and pretraining specialist Alexander Pritzel to Anthropic — joining Transformer co-author Noam Shazeer (now at OpenAI) and Nobel laureate John Jumper, the AlphaFold lead, who left for Anthropic earlier this month. Per TechCrunch and Bloomberg, that's four senior departures in roughly a week, and the AlphaFold core has essentially relocated across town.

The lure isn't a foosball table. Analysts tie the moves to pre-IPO equity: with OpenAI and Anthropic both circling public offerings, a grant signed today could be a yacht tomorrow. Google has the compute and Gemini's momentum; what it's losing is the irreplaceable part — the handful of people who know how to make the *next* model actually smarter.

**Bottom line:** Models are sliding toward commodity; the people who can build the next one are not. The pre-IPO equity bonfire is now the most effective recruiting tool in tech, and Google's the one getting singed.

## Stop copying our homework

Anthropic told U.S. senators that operators linked to Alibaba ran nearly 25,000 fake accounts to fire roughly 28.8 million questions at Claude between late April and early June — not to use it, but to harvest its answers and train a rival, per Global Banking & Finance. The technique is called distillation: a smaller "student" model learns by imitating a smarter "teacher's" outputs. Labs do it to their own models constantly; doing it to someone else's, against the rules, is the part Anthropic is calling theft. It says it's the largest such campaign it's caught, and ties it to earlier efforts it blames on DeepSeek and others. (Alibaba hasn't publicly responded; treat it as an accusation for now.)

**Bottom line:** The smartest models are now valuable enough to steal just by asking them millions of questions. The moat was never only the weights — it's the answers, and they leak one prompt at a time.

## The Big Picture

For three years the only question that mattered in AI was *can we build it.* This week the question quietly changed to *who's allowed to run it.* Two of the most capable models on earth are now gated by Washington, a third launched into a government-approved guest list, and the throughline is cyber: the same skill that lets a model patch a decades-old bug lets it pop a classified network, and there's no dial that delivers only the first. That nudges frontier AI toward something closer to controlled technology — export-controlled, citizenship-checked, access-by-policy — even as the compute beneath it (the chips, the data centers, the billion-dollar inference rounds) keeps scaling like nothing's wrong. The bet of the last cycle was that intelligence would get cheap and universal. The story of this week is that the smartest slice of it might not — and the people deciding aren't in the labs anymore.

## One More Thing

Spare a thought for the customer-service chatbot, unloved and besieged. In that Parloa survey of 1,001 Americans, 43.9% said they try to reach a human by simply repeating "human" or "person" into the phone like a magic incantation, 17% have resorted to swearing at the bot, and only 13.6% trust AI with anything complicated. The twist: 85% said they'd happily use an AI agent — *if* it actually solved their problem. So the machines aced a classified-network exam this week but still can't get you a refund. Priorities.
