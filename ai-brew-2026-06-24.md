## Banned, not beaten

On Tuesday, at the ISC supercomputing conference in Hamburg, the twice-yearly TOP500 ranking crowned a newcomer: **LineShine**, a Chinese system that clocked **2.198 exaflops** — a billion-billion calculations a second — to dethrone America's El Capitan. Two firsts make it sting in Washington. It's the first machine to *sustain* more than two exaflops, and it did so on entirely homegrown Chinese silicon — no Nvidia, Intel, or AMD inside. US export controls were built precisely to keep China off this frontier.

The catch: one trophy isn't the whole tournament. Nvidia chips still power more than 400 of the 500 fastest machines (81%), and LineShine leaned on a CPU-only design that's mighty but power-hungry.

**Bottom line:** Export controls slowed China's access to the best chips. They didn't stop China from building the biggest machine — it just built it without you.

## From pocket to power plant

The company whose chips sit in your phone wants a seat in the server room. Today, Qualcomm hosts its 2026 Investor Day, where CEO Cristiano Amon has promised to map growth across "gigawatt-scale data centers," physical AI, personal AI, and 6G. Wall Street wants specifics, not vibes: BofA expects Qualcomm to size its data-center opportunity at $2–5 billion by fiscal 2028, update a 200-megawatt accelerator deal with Saudi-backed HUMAIN, and detail its AI200 and AI250 inference chips — plus, investors hope, the name of a mystery hyperscaler customer.

Lurking in the background: reports from Reuters and The Information that Qualcomm is in talks to buy Jim Keller's chip startup Tenstorrent for $8–10 billion — a bet on open RISC-V designs aimed squarely at Nvidia's blind spot. The stock has rallied into the day.

**Bottom line:** Smartphone growth is flat, so Qualcomm is repitching itself as an AI-infrastructure company. Today it has to deliver names and numbers — not just slides.

## DeepMind, shallow bench

Google's AI lab is leaking stars. Per Axios and Fortune on Tuesday, DeepMind lost two heavyweights inside a single week: **Noam Shazeer** — Gemini co-lead and a co-author of the 2017 paper that invented the transformer, the "T" in ChatGPT — decamped to OpenAI, while Nobel laureate **John Jumper** (of AlphaFold fame) left for Anthropic. Investors flinched: Alphabet shares had their worst day in over a year on Monday, sliding about 6% on a tangle of talent and AI-spending worries.

The uncomfortable subtext is the pitch. OpenAI and Anthropic can dangle pre-IPO equity that an already-public Google can't match — the same Google that reportedly paid $2.7 billion two years ago to lure Shazeer back in the first place.

**Bottom line:** In the AI talent war, the hottest currency is stock in a company that hasn't gone public yet. That's a fight a public Google is structurally built to lose.

## Five Eyes, wide open

In a rare joint statement on Tuesday, the **Five Eyes** intelligence alliance — the US, UK, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand — warned that frontier AI could supercharge cyberattacks on governments, critical infrastructure, and major companies "within months," not years. The memo, titled "The AI shift in cyber risk: why leaders must act now," carries the signatures of six agency heads, including the US's NSA and CISA and the UK's NCSC. Their point cuts both ways: the same models that automate defense can automate offense — finding and exploiting vulnerabilities faster than humans can patch them. The advice was blunt — assume a breach is inevitable, and spend on recovery, not just walls.

**Bottom line:** The same agentic skills that let a model refactor your codebase let it probe someone else's. "AI security" just jumped from conference panel to board-level budget line — with a government deadline attached.

## ⚡ Quick Hits

🧠 **GPT-5.6 watch.** OpenAI's next model is widely expected this week — barely six weeks after 5.5 — and its chief scientist has already called it a "meaningful leap." Nothing's official; treat the leaked spec sheets as rumor until the system card lands (*Tech Times*).

💸 **Fable 5 hits the meter.** As of Tuesday, Anthropic's priciest model leaves subscription plans; pay-as-you-go runs $10 per million input tokens and $50 per million output — double Opus 4.8, and the most expensive frontier model Anthropic ships (*TechCrunch*).

🟢 **The US still owns the rest of the list.** Beneath China's #1, ISC numbers show Nvidia in 400+ of the TOP500 and the eight most energy-efficient machines, while AMD powers 4 of the top 10 and 191 systems overall, up 11% on the year.

🏛️ **Détente in D.C.** Trump told Axios he no longer views Anthropic as a national-security threat — "not now, but a week ago, maybe" — thawing the Fable 5 export spat after a G7 sit-down with CEO Dario Amodei.

📚 **Pens over prompts.** Norway is barring generative AI for kids in grades 1–7 (ages 6–13) from the fall term, part of a broader pivot back to books and away from tablets.

🧑‍🔬 **Brussels staffs up.** The European Commission named a 60-expert Scientific Panel this month to help enforce the AI Act — the referees are finally taking the field.

## 🔭 The Big Picture

Strip away the day's headlines and the same machine hums underneath all of them: the AI race has narrowed to a fight over a few bottlenecks — chips, brains, and trust — and all of them are getting more concentrated. China just proved it can build the world's biggest computer without American parts, even as Nvidia and Qualcomm scramble to extend their lead in the silicon that trains and runs the models. The scarcest resource of all — the handful of people who can design those models — is flowing toward whoever can offer pre-IPO upside. And the Five Eyes warning is a reminder that capability and danger scale together: every leap in what these models can build is a leap in what they can break. The takeaway for 2026: stop waiting for the one launch that "changes everything." The real contest is over who controls the chips, who employs the geniuses, and who's ready when the same tools get pointed the other way.

## 🎯 One More Thing

LineShine runs 2.198 exaflops — 2.2 billion-billion calculations every second. To match what it computes in *one second*, a human doing one sum per second, no sleep, no snacks, would need roughly **70 billion years** — about five times the current age of the universe. Your group chat's mental math is, statistically, doing just fine.
