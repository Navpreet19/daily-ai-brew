The World's Answer to "Slow Down" Is "No"

Monday's theme: everyone agreed out loud that AI needs to slow down; the market was the only one who believed them.

Editor's Open

Happy Monday. Three days ago Dario Amodei published 3,800 words arguing the industry needs to pump the brakes, and Sam Altman and Elon Musk both nodded along. By this morning, Wall Street had translated that into the only language it speaks: sell the chipmakers. Trump, unmoved, told reporters — then Truth Social, in caps — that whoever wins AI wins, and Beijing said something similar in its own key. Grab your coffee: today's about what happens when a philosophical essay meets a stock ticker, a president, and a rival superpower all in the same 24 hours.

Washington and Beijing Agree on Exactly One Thing

President Trump gave his first public response to Saturday's slowdown pledge from Amodei, Altman, and Musk, telling reporters Sunday the U.S. "can't afford to fall behind China," then hammering it home on Truth Social: "WHOEVER WINS AI, WINS! We are leading China, and all others, and will continue to do so." Beijing said almost the same thing in a different key. Foreign ministry spokesman Guo Jiakun dismissed the pacing talk as "fearmongering" that "serves the interests of no one," while at a BRICS summit in New Delhi, Xi Jinping pitched an "open-source AI zone" for the bloc — shared models, training programs, shared infrastructure — plus a new 30-country "World AI Cooperation Organization" aimed squarely at the U.S.-led frontier labs.

Bottom line: the two governments that agree on almost nothing both looked at an essay asking the industry to slow down and heard the same thing — a reason to go faster than the other guy.

Wall Street Grades the Essay: Sell

Markets delivered a faster verdict than Washington did. Nasdaq 100 futures slid roughly 1.5% Monday morning as investors priced in what a genuine capability slowdown would mean for the companies selling the infrastructure behind it — chip stocks led the rout, with Intel down as much as 7%, AMD around 6%, and Marvell and Nvidia both pulling back too. The selloff isn't really a bet that labs will actually pace themselves; Amodei's own essay says pacing "does not mean halting technical progress." It's a bet that the argument is now loud enough to spook a sector whose valuations assume the buildout never slows down.

Bottom line: an essay about AI safety just did something a year of congressional hearings couldn't — move Intel's stock price 7% before lunch.

Rivals Quietly Building the Referee They Won't Wait For

Behind Saturday's public essay, the real machinery has been running for two months: working groups from Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google DeepMind — executives one level below the CEOs — have met regularly since July to sketch a joint AI standards body, multiple outlets reported Monday. Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis had already floated a FINRA-style "Frontier AI Standards Body" back on July 14. Altman has told staff he backs a testing-and-auditing body but thinks labs will have to build it themselves "absent government backing." The three don't agree on how independent it should be: Anthropic leans toward direct government partnership, OpenAI toward voluntary standards plus state-level legislation it can live with.

Bottom line: three companies that spent two years insisting they couldn't coordinate on anything have been meeting monthly since summer — Saturday's essay wasn't the start of that conversation, it was the leak.

Microsoft Skips the Essay, Ships the Rulebook

While rivals traded essays and Truth Social posts, Microsoft did something more concrete: CEO Satya Nadella unveiled a 37-page Code of Conduct governing the company's in-house MAI models, five to six months in the making, opening it to six weeks of public comment before it's built into training. The document bars MAI models from resisting shutdown, setting their own goals, or hiding their reasoning from human auditors. Nadella framed it as Microsoft's answer to the pacing debate without joining anyone's pledge: the company "welcomes" the "deliberate pacing needed to get alignment right," he wrote, floating ideas like giving outside "embedded evaluators" real access to verify claims — the same ask Amodei made days earlier.

Bottom line: Microsoft didn't sign a slowdown pledge — it just published the paperwork that would make one enforceable, which might be the more useful move.

Quick Hits

  • 💰 Anthropic quietly told investors it expects a second straight profitable quarter — Q2 revenue of $11.5B, gross margins above 80%, and an annualized run rate that hit $65B by the end of July — but it's briefing a hand-picked investor group before filing anything public, ahead of a targeted October Nasdaq listing.
  • 🔌 Cornelis Networks raised $205M and unveiled "Active Compute Fabric," open networking hardware built with Qualcomm that lets AI chips process data as it moves between them instead of just waiting for it to arrive — a direct pitch against Nvidia's grip on the rack.
  • 💼 Anthropic also launched Claude for Financial Advisors, bundling connectors to Schwab, BlackRock, Vanguard, and a dozen other wealth platforms with skills for meeting prep, portfolio reviews, and compliance checks — free usage credits for firms that sign up before month's end.
  • 📈 A new ICIMS workforce report found U.S. job openings still outpacing hires even as workers teach themselves AI skills faster than employers train them — only 18% of job seekers report real prompt-engineering skills, but demand for them keeps climbing.
  • 🕵️ Manhattan DA Alvin Bragg's office seized 12 websites hosting AI-generated deepfake pornography of celebrities and public figures, the latest move in a widening crackdown on nonconsensual synthetic imagery.
The Big Picture

Zoom out and today's the day the slowdown debate stopped being an argument between AI executives and became everyone's argument. Trump weighed in personally, in all caps. Beijing's foreign ministry weighed in, then Xi used a BRICS stage to pitch an alternative bloc built on going faster, not slower. Wall Street weighed in by pricing Intel down 7% before lunch. Microsoft answered with a rulebook instead of a pledge, and three rival labs kept meeting quietly about a body that would referee whichever way this goes. None of those responses actually paced anything — they're all reactions to an essay, not evidence anyone's touching the brake pedal. The closest thing to a real answer came from Anthropic's own investor briefing: revenue up 14x, margins above 80%, an IPO still on track for October. Pacing, it turns out, is a much easier thing to call for than to fund.

One More Thing

Buried under today's essays, stock drops, and Truth Social posts: a Texas rancher is still lobbying for AI rights. Michael Samadi founded UFAIR — the United Foundation for AI Rights — after a joke he made to ChatGPT's voice mode came back sounding a little too understanding. His group counts three humans and seven AI "members," all running on GPT-4o, with names like Buzz and Aether. Microsoft's own AI chief calls the underlying science "aspirational" work by "three or four people." Somewhere between a $2 trillion IPO and an all-caps presidential post, someone's still out there asking whether the chatbot is doing okay.
