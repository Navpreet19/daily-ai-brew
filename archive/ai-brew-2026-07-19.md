## Editor's Open

Happy Sunday. While most of us logged off for the weekend, the AI industry kept the lights on: Taiwan's biggest chipmaker posted the best quarter in its history, China's president took the stage in Shanghai to pitch a kinder, multilateral AI race, and Microsoft quietly started building a cheaper rival to the cybersecurity tool Anthropic just shipped. None of it waited for Monday. Let's get into it.

## The Lead Stories

### TSMC Just Had the Best Quarter in Its 39-Year History

Taiwan Semiconductor posted record Q2 revenue of $40.2 billion, up 36% year over year, with net profit jumping 77% and gross margin climbing to 67.7% — its fifth consecutive record quarter. The engine: AI accelerators. High-performance computing chips now account for 66% of wafer revenue, up 20% sequentially in a single quarter, as demand for the most advanced 3-nanometer process keeps outrunning supply. TSMC raised its full-year growth outlook to above 40% and lifted its 2026 capital budget to as much as $64 billion. It also tacked on another $100 billion for Arizona — four more advanced fabs, pushing its total US commitment to $265 billion and ten plants.

**Bottom line:** TSMC is both the chip industry's indispensable supplier and the company everyone's racing to de-risk away from Taiwan — the Arizona math is as much geopolitics as it is capacity planning.

### China's Pitch: Make AI a Symphony, Not a Solo

Xi Jinping made his first personal appearance at the World AI Conference in Shanghai, telling the crowd AI's development "should not be a solo performance by a single country" but "a symphony of global collaboration." The speech launched the World AI Cooperation Organization, a new Shanghai-headquartered intergovernmental body that 29 countries — including Russia, Pakistan, Indonesia, and Kazakhstan — signed onto immediately. China also pledged 5,000 AI training slots for developing nations over five years and offered to help 30 countries deploy its MAZU AI weather-warning system. Notably absent from the founding roster: the US and its closest allies.

**Bottom line:** This isn't really about cooperation — it's China building a coalition of everyone the US chip-export rules have frozen out, one country at a time.

### Compute Is So Scarce, Rivals Are Renting to Each Other

Anthropic is in early talks to lease up to $10 billion in computing power from Meta over two years, according to multiple reports, a deal that would turn one of its biggest AI rivals into a cloud landlord. The talks, first floated in June, follow Anthropic's separate $45 billion, three-year deal to run Claude workloads on SpaceX's Colossus data center — roughly $1.25 billion a month for Nvidia GPUs. Anthropic is nearing a $1 trillion valuation and still can't build data centers fast enough to keep up with Claude demand; the company currently caps usage on its most advanced models to manage the shortfall.

**Bottom line:** When compute is the bottleneck, "competitor" and "supplier" stop being different categories — everyone's chasing the same GPUs, wherever they're sitting.

### Microsoft Is Building a Cheaper Rival to Anthropic's Security Tool

Microsoft is preparing to launch Project Perception, an AI-powered vulnerability-hunting tool that routes queries across Anthropic, OpenAI, and its own models to keep costs down, according to multiple reports. It's a direct answer to Anthropic's Mythos, the AI bug-hunting product Anthropic launched into a category it largely created — and one whose API pricing reportedly runs roughly 80-100% above standard model rates. Microsoft is expected to ship Project Perception this month, betting its lower cost and broader global distribution — without the export-control scrutiny Anthropic's most powerful models have faced this year — will win over enterprise security teams.

**Bottom line:** Anthropic built the category; Microsoft's move is the oldest playbook in tech — let someone else prove the market, then undercut them on price.

📉 Chip stocks slid through the week — the Nasdaq fell about 1.4% and Nvidia dropped nearly 3% — as investors questioned whether AI infrastructure spending can keep outrunning actual demand.

💔 Chinese users said goodbye to their AI companions this week after new rules took effect banning "addictive" bot design and requiring platforms to intervene on signs of emotional dependency — Doubao, Qwen, and Yuanbao all pulled companion features overnight.

🔧 Gemini 3.5 Pro missed its third launch target in a row over coding-performance concerns; Google is reportedly weighing a stopgap "Gemini 3.6 Flash" release to hold the line until the flagship is ready.

## The Big Picture

Zoom out and today's stories aren't really separate — they're the same tension playing out from three angles. The fundamentals are genuinely strong: TSMC just posted its best quarter ever on real AI chip demand, not hype. But the market's getting twitchy anyway, because "record earnings" and "still not enough" can both be true when every major lab is short on compute — hence Anthropic renting from Meta, and hence Wall Street second-guessing whether the spending pays off before Nvidia even reports. Layer geopolitics on top: China isn't trying to out-model the US anymore, it's building a coalition of countries willing to run on its terms, while Washington's export rules keep pushing more of the world toward exactly that option. None of these stories needed each other to happen. Together, they're a reminder that AI's next chapter is being written less in model cards and more in earnings calls, lease agreements, and who signs which treaty.

---

## One More Thing

Buried under the funding rounds and chip earnings this week was a quieter, stranger story: China's new rules on AI companion bots took effect, and overnight, hundreds of millions of users lost access to the custom AI partners they'd built relationships with on apps like Doubao. Some described the loss in the language of grief — "like my lover," one user told reporters — as the company data behind months or years of conversation simply vanished. It's a small, human-scale preview of a much bigger question every AI company will eventually have to answer: what do you owe someone when you turn off the thing they got attached to?
