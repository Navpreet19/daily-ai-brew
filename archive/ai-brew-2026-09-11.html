Friday's theme: half the industry spent the week warning the world might end, and the other half spent it negotiating the price of the chips anyway.

Editor's Open

Happy Friday. A researcher who spent three years building the stuff quit loudly enough to rack up 150 million views warning it could kill everyone, the Pentagon spent Thursday explaining why it's not worried, and OpenAI spent the same week quietly asking Congress whether it's even legal to slow down on purpose. Grab your coffee — today's about what happens when an industry's own insiders stop agreeing on the story, and everyone downstream has to pick a side anyway.

A Tweet Sent the Pentagon Into Its Feelings

Jacob Coxon, who spent three years on pretraining teams at OpenAI and Anthropic, resigned this week and posted that AI labs are "racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives" — a message now past 150 million views. Two of Anthropic's own safety leads, Evan Hubinger and Samuel Marks, publicly backed him, with Hubinger putting the odds of AI killing everyone above 10% within a decade. Washington's answer came fast: Pentagon CTO Emil Michael dismissed it as a "doom loop" at a defense conference and revealed the Pentagon has already shifted 90% of its classified AI workload off Anthropic, with the rest due by month's end. Trump waved off extinction risk outright, and Nvidia's Jensen Huang called it "complete nonsense."

Bottom line: when the people building the technology and the people buying it for the U.S. military can't agree on whether it might end humanity, "trust the experts" stops being useful advice — the experts disagree with each other.

OpenAI Wants a Permission Slip to Slow Down

Sam Altman told OpenAI staff this week the company is open to slowing development of its most advanced models, ideally alongside rivals — a real shift for a CEO who's spent two years insisting the race can't pause. The catch: OpenAI has reportedly spent weeks quietly asking members of Congress whether coordinating a slowdown with competitors would even be legal, wary that an industry-wide pause could look like Sherman Act price-fixing dressed up as safety. The question landed the same week Anthropic's own researchers went public with extinction-level fears and the Pentagon publicly shrugged them off.

Bottom line: "can we legally agree to slow down" being a live question in Washington means the AI race stopped being just a technology story months ago — it's an antitrust story now too.

Anthropic Says Its Rivals Were Wearing a Trenchcoat

Anthropic accused Chinese labs DeepSeek and Moonshot of secretly routing customer requests to Claude and serving the answers back as their own — "illicit distillation," in Anthropic's phrasing, not just training on old outputs but running live traffic through a rival's model without telling users. In one 10-day window, Anthropic says Moonshot relayed nearly 300,000 requests to Claude Opus; over 14 days in July it logged more than 12 million distillation attempts tied to DeepSeek alone, some carrying sensitive data from named companies and state-linked actors. It's the sharpest evidence yet behind this week's NSA/CISA/FBI advisory naming six Chinese labs for the same practice.

Bottom line: training on a rival's outputs after the fact is one thing; quietly routing a rival's model your users' live requests and letting them think you answered is another, and Anthropic just published the receipts.

Two Governments Just Co-Signed Cohere's Series E

Toronto-based Cohere is in advanced talks to raise $2-3 billion at a $20 billion valuation, Bloomberg and the Globe and Mail reported, with Canada's government and existing backers joined by Germany's — the same round that folds in Cohere's previously announced merger with Germany's Aleph Alpha. It would be the largest round ever raised by a private Canadian company, and could close as soon as next week. The structure makes literal what Mistral's Samsung-led raise implied last week: "sovereign AI" pitches increasingly come with actual sovereigns writing checks, not just customers who sound patriotic in press releases.

Bottom line: when two national governments co-sign your Series E, you've stopped selling AI as a product and started selling it as infrastructure policy — a stickier, more political business to be in.

Quick Hits

  • 🖥️ Google shipped a native Gemini app for Windows with an Alt+Space shortcut that summons the assistant over any open program, plus built-in Nano Banana image generation and Gemini Omni video.
  • 🤝 Meta acquired Stockholm-based agent startup Stilla, eight months after it launched, to power Meta Business Agent's WhatsApp, Instagram, and Messenger commerce tools already used by over 1 million businesses.
  • 💾 A global HBM shortage pushed Chinese AI chip prices up 20-50% since June — Huawei's Ascend 950DT now runs above 250,000 yuan (~$37,000), with Cambricon, MetaX, and Iluvatar CoreX all repricing higher too.
  • 🎓 A 35-country survey of over 45,000 students and faculty found 88% of students now use AI in coursework, up 16 points from last year — though 57% say they still lack adequate guidance on how.
  • 💳 OpenAI paused new $200/month ChatGPT Pro sign-ups, citing "unprecedented" demand strain since GPT-6 Astra's launch, while separately signing a 27-month, fee-free GSA deal opening discounted ChatGPT access to federal, state, local, and tribal government workers.
  • 🔌 Nvidia's Groq 3 LPU inference chip entered full production as part of the Vera Rubin platform rollout — folding the technology from its DOJ-scrutinized Groq licensing deal directly into its next-gen lineup.
The Big Picture

Zoom out and this week's throughline isn't AI getting more dangerous — it's the industry's trust scaffolding buckling in public, at every altitude. A researcher stopped trusting his own employer's roadmap enough to quit loudly; the Pentagon stopped trusting that same employer's models enough to route around them entirely; Anthropic stopped trusting two Chinese rivals to tell their own users where their answers actually come from; and two national governments decided they trust a single Canadian startup enough to co-sign its balance sheet. None of that required a new model or a new capability — just people, companies, and governments updating, in public, who they believe. That's a more contagious kind of story than any benchmark, because unlike a benchmark, trust doesn't come back just because someone issues a statement saying it should.

One More Thing

Buried under today's extinction debate: Nvidia's Jensen Huang offered the least reassuring reassurance of the week. Dismissing AI doom fears as "complete nonsense," he then said the quiet part out loud: "what better way to create demand than to create a problem... who doesn't want their market to be hysterical about their product and line up around the corner for it?" Somewhere, a marketing team is taking notes on how to turn an extinction warning into a sales funnel.
