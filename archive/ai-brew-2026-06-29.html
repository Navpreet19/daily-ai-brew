Good morning. The long June weekend is over, and while you were at the grill, the AI labs were too — searing chips, plating new models, and quietly stacking one very large legal headache. The week's throughline: the companies sprinting toward artificial general intelligence increasingly want to own every layer of the stack — and decide who's allowed to stand on it. Let's dig in.

Sol food

OpenAI on Friday previewed GPT-5.6, a three-model family named Sol, Terra, and Luna (yes, like a yoga studio). Sol is the frontier brain, tuned for long coding sessions, deep reasoning, and agent work; Terra matches last generation's GPT-5.5 at roughly half the price; Luna is the fast, cheap runt of the litter. But the real news isn't the model — it's the door policy. Per Axios and VentureBeat, OpenAI shared GPT-5.6 with the U.S. government ahead of release and is handing it to only about 20 trusted organizations at first, citing safety and "higher-risk" misuse. General availability is "coming weeks" away. Which means the marquee figures — Sol at $5 in / $30 out per million tokens — matter less right now than the velvet rope in front of them.

Bottom line: When a frontier model launches on a government-vetted guest list, the product isn't just intelligence — it's the question of who's trusted to hold it.

Pass the Jalapeño

Days earlier, OpenAI and Broadcom unveiled Jalapeño, OpenAI's first custom chip — an inference processor (the part that actually answers you, as opposed to training the model) built from scratch in about nine months. OpenAI claims meaningfully better performance-per-watt than today's best silicon and says early samples are already running its GPT-5.3-Codex-Spark model in testing, per TechCrunch, with a data-center rollout slated for late 2026. Translation: the company that buys mountains of Nvidia GPUs would very much like to stop. Designing your own application-specific chip is how you escape both the price tag and the waitlist.

Bottom line: Owning the model and the chip it runs on is OpenAI's bid to control its own costs — and a sign that Nvidia's biggest customers are quietly turning into its competitors.

Distill, my beating heart

Anthropic went public last week with a pointed accusation: that operators tied to Alibaba's Qwen lab ran roughly 25,000 fraudulent accounts to query Claude 28.8 million times between late April and early June, harvesting its answers to train a rival. That's a distillation attack — copying a smarter model's homework, not stealing its source code or weights. Anthropic laid it out in a letter to U.S. senators and flagged the White House, per CNBC, calling it the largest such campaign it has seen. Alibaba hasn't publicly responded, and "alleged" is doing real work here: this is Anthropic's telling, not a court's finding.

Bottom line: Model outputs are the new trade secret, and the U.S.–China AI fight is migrating from chip-export rules to the answers chatbots give away for free.

Press start to continue

On the lighter end of the ledger, General Intuition unveiled a $320M Series A at a $2.3B valuation — led by Khosla Ventures, with Jeff Bezos and Eric Schmidt along for the ride — to teach AI by feeding it millions of hours of video-game clips. The bet, per TechCrunch: today's models have learned to see the world; the harder, more valuable trick is predicting what happens when you act in it — physics, consequences, intuition — and gamers generate exactly that data, by the petabyte, via the clip-sharing app Medal. The prize they're chasing is robots and agents that move through reality like a speedrunner threads a level.

Bottom line: The next great training set may not be the internet's text — it might be your gaming highlight reel.

The Big Picture

Zoom out and the week tells a single story: AI is finishing its transition from software to infrastructure — and from infrastructure to statecraft. The labs now want the whole stack — the model, the chip it runs on, the data center it lives in, and increasingly a say in which governments and companies get access at all. That's why OpenAI is fabbing its own silicon, why a model launch suddenly routes through Washington, and why a scraping campaign gets escalated to the Senate instead of a legal department. Compute is capital, model outputs are secrets, and access is policy. The hype cycle made AI feel like apps; 2026 is making it feel like oil, fabs, and treaties.

One More Thing

Spare a thought for the gamers. Somewhere out there, a teenager's 3 a.m. clutch in a shooter is being vacuumed up to teach a warehouse robot how to pivot around a corner. They never signed up to train our future mechanical coworkers — but they did post the clip. Consider it the first rule of the AI era: if it's online and it's interesting, it's a dataset. Touch grass while you still can.
