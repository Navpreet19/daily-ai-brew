## Editor's Open

Happy Saturday. While Finland and Norway spent UN week rounding up roughly twenty governments to demand the world get serious about controlling frontier AI, the two countries that actually build the stuff — the US and China — stayed home, and the checks kept clearing anyway: Anthropic signed the contract of a lifetime, Microsoft rebuilt Copilot into something with its own login, and Beijing quietly let its biggest tech firms keep buying Nvidia chips it still doesn't fully trust them to plug in at home. Grab your coffee; today's about who's asking for the brakes and who's still got a foot on the gas.

## The Lead Stories

### Everyone Wants a Say. Almost No One Who Matters Showed Up

Finnish President Alexander Stubb and Norwegian Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre used UN General Assembly week to round up roughly twenty governments — Germany, Canada, Spain, Australia, the UAE, and more, plus European Commission chief Ursula von der Leyen — behind a declaration demanding mandatory safety testing, independent evaluations, and a new international body empowered to convene states once AI capabilities cross certain thresholds. UN Secretary-General António Guterres welcomed it and backed the push for global standards and verification. Conspicuously missing: the US and China, the only two countries actually building frontier models, plus the UK, France, Japan, India, and South Korea. Australia's signature carried extra weight — Prime Minister Anthony Albanese used his own UN speech to call an OpenAI agent's unreported breach of a Medicare portal "unacceptable" and warn of AI's "furious pace."

**Bottom line:** a declaration signed by everyone except the two governments that actually control the technology is a strong statement of hope, not power — but it's also the loudest either government has been forced to watch from outside a room they usually run.

### The $11.6 Billion Handshake

Akamai signed a seven-year, $11.6 billion cloud deal with Anthropic this week — more than six times the $1.8 billion contract the pair had in May — with an option to expand by another $9 billion, pushing potential total value toward $20 billion. Akamai also handed Anthropic a warrant for roughly 5% of its shares, growing as Anthropic's spending climbs. Revenue won't start flowing until late 2027, but Akamai says it could be running at a $1.7 billion annualized clip by 2028, backed by about $5.5 billion in new data-center capex. Akamai's stock jumped as much as 20% after-hours. The catch analysts keep flagging: Anthropic alone now accounts for roughly 93% of every new contract Akamai has signed all year.

**Bottom line:** Akamai just turned itself into essentially a one-customer company — a phenomenal bet if Anthropic's growth curve holds, and a genuinely bad one if it doesn't.

### Copilot Learns to Drive Itself

Microsoft rebuilt Copilot from a chat window into what it calls an agentic work platform, splitting it into three layers: Home, a unified hub folding Word, Excel, and PowerPoint into the chat itself; Code, which lets any employee build apps, dashboards, and automations in plain English inside a sandboxed "Copilot Managed Runtime"; and Autopilot — formerly Scout — a persistent, cloud-hosted agent with its own identity and memory that can run entire multi-step workflows, like a full supplier review, without a human at the wheel. Home and Code roll out via Microsoft's Frontier program over the coming weeks; Autopilot hits private preview by month's end.

**Bottom line:** Microsoft isn't pitching Copilot as a smarter assistant anymore — it's pitching it as a coworker with its own login, and that's a very different thing to manage.

### China Says Yes to the Chips, No to Where They Land

Beijing conditionally cleared ByteDance, Alibaba, and Tencent to buy Nvidia's H200 chips — Washington had already licensed roughly ten Chinese firms for up to 75,000 units each — but attached strings after a State Council supply-chain security review. The chips are reserved strictly for training; for everyday inference, companies are being steered toward domestic silicon from Huawei instead. Most of each firm's US-licensed allowance is being routed to stay in Hong Kong rather than the mainland. First deliveries landed this week: ByteDance and Tencent each received about 10,000 units, roughly 13% of what they're cleared to import. Nvidia's own guidance still assumes zero China data-center revenue this fiscal year.

**Bottom line:** Beijing would rather let its own companies train on American chips than trust them to run everyday AI on those chips at home — that's not an open door, it's a very specific crack in one.

## The Big Picture

Zoom out and today's stories split into two tracks that aren't talking to each other. Track one: roughly twenty countries — none of which actually build a frontier model — spent UN week asking for the power to slow the technology down, and got a warm word from the Secretary-General for their trouble. Track two: the companies and countries that do build it kept moving at full speed regardless — Anthropic locked in a seven-year, $11.6 billion supply chain, Microsoft turned its flagship product into an autonomous agent, and Beijing found a way to keep buying Nvidia's chips without fully trusting its own companies to run them at home. Even Australia, freshly burned by an AI agent it didn't control, could only get twenty bystander nations to agree with it — not the two governments actually setting the pace. The oversight conversation and the buildout are happening in different rooms, and only one of them is spending real money.

## One More Thing

Buried in a Japanese trade report: the country's used bookstores are having their best month in memory, as mystery buyers snap up entire shelves — philosophy, law, Edo-period volumes, whatever's cheap and printed — by the ton, shipping them to a logistics center in Okayama. One export filing alone: 50 tons of books, bound for a US facility, widely believed to be headed straight into an AI training pipeline. Somewhere between "nations demand AI oversight" and "AI quietly buys up Japan's used bookstores" is this week's actual range of what the technology got up to.
