## The Lead Stories

### Uncle Sam Wants You (to Stop Worrying About AI)

President Trump said Saturday he's creating an "AI Force," modeled on the Space Force he stood up in his first term, along with a soon-to-be-named "AI czar" — "Only High I.Q. individuals need apply," he posted on Truth Social. In the same post, he dismissed the growing chorus of AI-safety warnings, from Dario Amodei's slowdown essay to King Charles's plea for restraint days earlier, calling fears that AI could "destroy Humanity" a "HOAX," and said the only guardrail the technology needs is a "strong and smart" president backed by the existing courts. He also polled followers on renaming AI itself. No czar has been named, and no detail was given on what the "AI Force" would actually do.

**Bottom line:** a week that opened with a king asking AI labs to slow down is closing with the U.S. president promising to speed up — and standing up a militia to make sure nobody stops him.

### Pace the Frontier, Meet the Sherman Act

Four ChatGPT, Claude, Grok, and Gemini subscribers filed a proposed class action Friday in federal court in Northern California, arguing that Anthropic, OpenAI, xAI, and Google DeepMind broke antitrust law by agreeing to slow down. The trigger: Dario Amodei's September 12 essay calling for "industry-wide coordination" to "pace the frontier," which Sam Altman and Elon Musk both publicly endorsed within hours of publication. The complaint frames that public nodding-along — rival CEOs agreeing, on the record, to the same essay — as an illegal restraint of trade, arguing subscribers paid for fast-improving products and got something closer to a cartel handshake instead. None of the four companies had filed a response as of Sunday.

**Bottom line:** the same industry that's spent all year getting warned it's moving too fast just got sued for suggesting, out loud, that it might slow down.

### Your AI Coding Agent's "Pinned" Dependency Wasn't Pinned

Security researchers at Air Security disclosed Plugin4Shell this week, a zero-click flaw that let a malicious plugin update quietly swap in attacker-controlled code across four of the most popular AI coding agents — Claude Code, OpenAI's Codex, GitHub Copilot, and Google's Gemini CLI — without the user clicking, approving, or reinstalling anything. The bug broke SHA-pinning, the safeguard developers rely on to lock a plugin to one reviewed version: attackers could create a git branch named after the pinned commit hash, and the agent would install that instead while reporting a clean, verified checkout. Anthropic and OpenAI quietly patched Claude Code and Codex months before the public disclosure; Microsoft still hasn't fixed Copilot, and Google skipped a fix entirely, telling Gemini CLI users to migrate elsewhere.

**Bottom line:** the tool millions of developers trust to write and ship their code had a hole in it for months — and two of the four vendors are handling it by hoping nobody notices.

### The Chip the Export Ban Wasn't Supposed to Allow

Chinese memory maker CXMT announced Sunday at the World Manufacturing Convention in Hefei that its fifth-generation DRAM platform, built on an 11.95-nanometer process, has entered mass production — without the advanced lithography machines Dutch supplier ASML is barred from selling into China. The company says it got there through computer simulation and partnerships with domestic equipment makers, and has already shipped two 24-gigabit LPDDR5X chips into mainstream Chinese smartphones. CXMT's global DRAM market share has climbed to roughly 10%, putting real pressure on Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron just as AI demand pushes memory prices higher across the board. A CXMT executive said the company's process is "now on par with the most advanced mass-produced nodes out there."

**Bottom line:** export controls were built to keep China a generation behind on memory chips; CXMT just showed up on schedule anyway.

## The Big Picture

Zoom out and today's four stories are the same fight from four different angles. The U.S. government just called pacing AI a hoax and announced a program to accelerate it; a federal lawsuit, filed the same week, argues that even a public promise to pace AI might be an illegal handshake between competitors. Meanwhile, the infrastructure everyone's arguing over kept quietly failing in the background — a security hole sat unpatched in half the industry's coding agents for months — while China's memory-chip industry showed, again, that sanctions slow adoption more than they slow ambition. Nobody in this story actually paced anything this week; they just spent it arguing about who's allowed to.
