## The Lead Stories

### Xi Steps Onto the AI Stage — and Brings 28 Friends

Xi Jinping delivered his first-ever in-person keynote at Shanghai's World AI Conference on Friday, his debut at an event China has run since 2018, calling for AI development to be "a symphony of international cooperation, not a solo performance by a single country." The timing wasn't subtle: a day earlier, 29 countries — including Russia, Brazil, Pakistan, Indonesia, Cuba, Venezuela, Belarus, Serbia, Kazakhstan, and Laos — signed a treaty in Shanghai establishing the World AI Cooperation Organization, a new intergovernmental body headquartered in the city. China's foreign minister Wang Yi signed on Beijing's behalf, and UN Secretary-General António Guterres attended the ceremony. Analysts read the new body as a governance alternative pitched at the Global South, outside the EU's AI Act and the G7's frameworks — and notably, without the US or its closest allies in the room. The conference runs through Monday with 140-plus forums and 1,100 exhibitors.

**Bottom line:** Washington didn't lose a vote today — it just wasn't invited to one, and 29 governments showed up anyway.

### TSMC Cashes Its Best Quarter Ever, Then Writes an Even Bigger Check

TSMC's Q2 numbers landed Thursday and beat expectations on every line: $40.2 billion in revenue (about $900 million above estimates), GAAP earnings per share of $4.31 (30 cents ahead of forecasts), and net profit up roughly 77% year-over-year, with gross margin at 67.7% — above the top of its own guidance. AI-driven high-performance computing chips now make up 66% of total revenue. On the same earnings call, TSMC pledged another $100 billion toward four new Arizona fabs focused on 2-nanometer chips and advanced packaging, pushing its total US commitment to $265 billion. Wall Street's reaction: sell anyway. The Philadelphia semiconductor index dropped 3.5% and the Nasdaq 100 fell 1.2% as Nvidia, Micron, AMD, and TSMC itself all slid, with investors questioning whether trillions in AI capex commitments can actually pay off.

**Bottom line:** When the best quarter in company history can't move the stock up, the market isn't grading TSMC anymore — it's grading everyone's AI bet at once.

### Nvidia Turns a Japan Trip Into a Robot Coalition

Jensen Huang spent two days in Japan this week turning the country into Nvidia's newest physical-AI showcase. Nvidia and local partner Noetra Corp. are building a Vera Rubin AI factory packing 13,750 Vera CPUs and 27,500 Rubin GPUs, with Fujitsu, Hitachi, and Kawasaki Heavy Industries joining the coalition around it. Alongside the trip, Nvidia unveiled Cosmos 3 Edge, a new open "world model" that helps robots and vision-AI agents perceive and navigate real environments in real time — and pointed to Boston Dynamics, Caterpillar, Franka Robotics, and NEURA Robotics as partners already building on its Jetson-and-Omniverse robotics stack. Nvidia is also leaning into a new pitch: its Vera CPU line represents a fresh $200 billion market it hasn't touched before, with management citing "visibility to almost $20 billion" in CPU revenue this year alone.

**Bottom line:** Nvidia isn't just selling the brains for AI anymore — it wants to sell the body, the factory, and the country-scale contract that puts robots to work in it.

### Two Labs, One Day Apart, Just Released the Biggest Open Models Ever

Mira Murati's Thinking Machines Lab dropped its first model, Inkling, on Wednesday — an open-weight, 975-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts system (41 billion active per task) trained on 45 trillion tokens of text, image, audio, and video, with a 1-million-token context window. It's the largest open-weight model any American lab has released — though Thinking Machines is refreshingly blunt that it's "not the strongest overall model available today," betting instead that companies will pay for one they can actually customize. One day later, China's Moonshot AI answered with Kimi K3: a 2.8-trillion-parameter open model, the largest ever released, period, using a new attention mechanism that decodes up to 6.3x faster at million-token context. It's already live in the Kimi app, with full open weights promised by July 27, and early benchmarks show it beating Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 on several evals.

**Bottom line:** The closed frontier gets the headlines, but the open-weight race just doubled in size in 24 hours — and the biggest model in it still isn't American.

## The Big Picture

Zoom out and today's stories are really one story: who gets to define what AI becomes, told across four battlegrounds. Politically, China just gave 29 countries a seat at a table the West didn't build. Financially, record AI profits no longer guarantee a rising stock — investors want proof the spending pays off, not just bigger numbers. Physically, Nvidia is racing to own the robots and factories that turn AI into something that moves in the real world. And technically, the fight over the best open model just crossed the trillion-parameter mark, with China still setting the pace. None of these fights particularly needs the others to be won — but together they're the clearest sign yet that AI's next chapter isn't about any one model. It's about who controls the rails underneath all of them.

## One More Thing

Kimi K3 launched with genuinely strong benchmarks — and also got tested, as every serious model now must, on Simon Willison's pelican benchmark: "generate an SVG of a pelican riding a bicycle." The verdict? It burned 13,241 reasoning tokens to produce a pelican that cost about 25 cents to draw. For context, just saying "hi" to the model burns 86 tokens, suggesting an 85-token hidden system prompt fires before it even says hello. Somewhere, a very expensive bird is riding a very cheap bicycle.
