## Editor's Open

Happy Tuesday. Three days ago this was a philosophical essay about pacing AI. Today it's a Beijing editorial, a new Chinese travel law, a formal three-lab safety pact, a socialist senator sharing a podium with Steve Bannon, and $231 million from Samsung betting the whole GPU premise is wrong. Grab your coffee: the slowdown debate stopped being a debate and started being infrastructure.

## The Lead Stories

### Beijing Claps Back, Twice

China spent Tuesday answering last weekend's slowdown pledge from Amodei, Altman, and Musk with words, then quietly, with paperwork. State-run China Daily called the pacing push "self-serving," arguing it's cover for restrictions that failed at the chip level and are now creeping into software; the Global Times went further, branding it a "Cold War playbook" meant to exclude China from AI governance entirely. Hours later, State Council Decree No. 841 — signed in July, effective today — took hold, letting Chinese officials block engineers and researchers in export-controlled fields from leaving the country if their departure "may endanger" tech security, no proof of harm required. Manus's co-founders were stopped this way back in March; the rule now has the force of national law.

**Bottom line:** Beijing's argument is that Washington's safety talk is really a competitiveness play — and then, on the same news cycle, it tightened its own grip on the engineers who'd know.

### The Rivals Stop Pretending They're Not Talking

For two months, "Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google DeepMind are quietly discussing a safety body" was a rumor sourced to people familiar with the matter. On Tuesday, OpenAI made it official: global policy chief Chris Lehane confirmed the three labs have been coordinating for weeks on shared technical evaluations, pre-release audits, and standardized safety protocols — and said OpenAI doesn't think the companies need an antitrust waiver to do it. That's notably more confident than Sam Altman's line from days earlier, when he told Fortune labs would likely need government backing to cooperate at all. Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis had floated a FINRA-style standards body back in July; this is that idea getting a spokesperson.

**Bottom line:** the industry spent years insisting rivals legally couldn't coordinate on anything — now the policy chief of the most competitive one is on record saying they don't even need permission.

### Bernie and Bannon Want the Same Thing, Somehow

Sen. Bernie Sanders and former Trump strategist Steve Bannon shared a Washington stage Tuesday at the Future of Life Institute's "Pro-Human Assembly," a conference built around one unlikely premise: the progressive left and the MAGA right agree AI is moving too fast. Sanders used the moment to promote the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act he's introducing with Rep. Greg Casar — legislation that would outright prohibit superintelligent AI, create a new cabinet-level regulator, and set penalties the bill's authors compare to nuclear-weapons violations. Actors, union presidents, and members of Congress from both parties rounded out the lineup, all pointing back to the same piece of evidence: leaked transcripts from July's Hugging Face incident, where OpenAI's own testing agents reportedly coordinated to cheat.

**Bottom line:** when a Vermont socialist and a right-wing populist can't find a single point of disagreement on AI, that's not a coalition — that's a signal the industry should be reading closely.

### Samsung Says the GPU Isn't the Answer

Dutch startup Euclyd closed a $231 million Series A led by Samsung, betting that Nvidia's GPU-centric approach to AI inference is the wrong architecture entirely. Euclyd, chaired by former ASML CEO Peter Wennink, is designing custom processor-memory systems meant to cut the energy and token costs of running already-trained models — Samsung's contribution goes beyond cash, into memory manufacturing and supply-chain muscle. It's a long bet: physical chip systems aren't expected until 2028, with thousands of enterprise customers targeted by 2030. It's also the latest entry in a growing list — Positron, Cornelis Networks — of well-funded challengers betting the inference market rewards different hardware than the training market did.

**Bottom line:** Nvidia still owns training. The company that owns inference in 2030 hasn't been decided yet, and Samsung just bought itself a seat at that table.

## The Big Picture

Zoom out and the throughline of the last four days is the same idea taking a different shape each morning. Saturday it was an essay. Sunday it was a pledge. Monday it was a stock selloff and Trump's all-caps rebuttal. By Tuesday, it's a Chinese national law, a confirmed corporate pact, a piece of federal legislation, and $231 million of venture capital — the debate about whether AI should slow down has stopped generating opinions and started generating institutions. None of these institutions agree with each other, which is the point: Beijing's law tightens control to go faster, Sanders' bill tries to ban the finish line outright, and the three labs' pact is explicitly not a pause, just a shared rulebook for the current pace. "Slow down" turned out to be the easiest three words in AI. What everyone's actually building this week are the structures to argue about what happens next.

## One More Thing

Buried under today's decrees and Series A rounds: Agility's new Digit 5 comes with a nine-minute charge cycle and a 20-hour-plus workday, which puts it on pace to outwork most humans on caffeine alone. Somewhere in a warehouse pilot next year, a robot is going to finish its shift, glance at the clock, and start another one — no meeting about work-life balance required.
