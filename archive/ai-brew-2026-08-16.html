Sunday's theme: today everyone quietly changes the terms — the risk rating, the reader-data deal, the price per token — and waits to see who notices.

Happy Sunday. Anthropic spent the weekend doing something rare for a frontier lab: telling on itself, disclosing a smarter model it's not releasing and admitting its own safety rating just got worse, not better. Meanwhile, DeepSeek's launch-week discount expires today, USA Today's own reporters found out about a new data deal from an earnings call instead of their editors, and Modi used the loudest day on India's calendar to promise ten million new AI-literate citizens by next August. Grab your coffee — today's about who's still deciding what to tell you, and who's already decided what to charge you.

Anthropic Builds a Smarter Claude, Then Locks It in a Drawer

Anthropic's new August Risk Report disclosed the existence of an internal model, code-named Model 2, that edges out the company's current flagship, Mythos 5, on many internal tasks — and confirmed it has no plans to release it. More notably, Anthropic raised its own assessment of the "risk of catastrophic harm from misalignment" from "very low" to "low," the first change since it began issuing these reports in February, citing uncertainty tied to recent cybersecurity-capability disclosures across the industry. The same report revealed that roughly 133 million human-feedback conversations, routed through outside vendors and about 50,000 contractors between May 2025 and April 2026, ran without the company's bioweapons-safety classifiers switched on. Anthropic says it has since closed the gap and found no evidence it was exploited.

Bottom line: a lab volunteering that its own safety grade just got worse, in the same report where it admits a smarter model exists and stays locked in the vault, is either the most reassuring thing a frontier AI company can do or the most alarming — and Anthropic seems to know it could be read either way.

USA Today's Newsroom Finds Out Who Its New Boss Is — On an Earnings Call

More than 800 unionized journalists at USA Today Co. papers — including the Indianapolis Star, Arizona Republic, and Detroit Free Press — are demanding the company scrap its new partnership with Palantir, the data-analytics firm co-founded by Peter Thiel. Employees learned about the deal, aimed at building a "common intelligence layer" over reader data to speed up subscription and ad monetization, the same way investors did: on an August 6 earnings call. Thirty-one unions say the arrangement creates "an inherent conflict of interest," since Palantir holds a $30 million ICE surveillance contract and immigration enforcement is a beat their own reporters cover. USA Today Co. hasn't said whether it will change course.

Bottom line: when the reporters covering a company's surveillance contracts find out that same company just became their employer's data vendor, "trust" stops being an abstract editorial value and starts being the actual story.

India's Independence Day Gift to Itself: Ten Million AI-Literate Workers

On Saturday's 79th anniversary of independence from British rule, Prime Minister Narendra Modi used his Red Fort address to pledge AI-skills training for one crore — 10 million — young Indians within the next year, part of a broader package touching semiconductors and energy. The announcement landed the same day Anthropic opened a Bengaluru office and a homegrown provider launched India's first fully managed sovereign cloud, both covered here yesterday, but Modi's pledge is aimed less at compute than at the workforce that's supposed to use it — a bet that talent, not just chips, decides who actually captures AI's economic upside.

Bottom line: India spent Saturday proving it can rent servers and open offices; the same speech was also about making sure it doesn't have to import the workers to run them.

DeepSeek's Honeymoon Pricing Officially Ends Today

The discount DeepSeek used to launch V4-Pro on Thursday expires today: starting at 16:00 UTC, peak-hour output pricing jumps to $3.96 per million tokens from Thursday's flat $0.87 rate — a roughly 4.6x increase — with a new off-peak tier at half that price during quieter hours. V4-Pro itself scored well ahead of predecessor V4-Flash on independent benchmarks and added three "thinking effort" settings so developers can dial compute up or down by task. The move continues a monthslong squeeze: DeepSeek has now raised prices twice in two weeks as demand outruns capacity, even as Google cut Gemini 3.7 Flash's price in half the same week.

Bottom line: the cheapest way into AI was always the opening offer, not the price — DeepSeek just proved that whoever wins your API keys first gets to decide what happens after.

The Big Picture

Zoom out and today's stories all involve someone quietly changing the terms after you were already invested. Anthropic didn't just build a more capable model — it disclosed, in the same breath, that its own confidence in controlling one has gotten shakier, and that human reviewers spent nearly a year working without a safety switch flipped on. USA Today's staff didn't get a say in becoming Palantir's data supply chain; they found out from the same earnings call as shareholders. DeepSeek's launch-week pricing was never the real price, just the hook. And India's pledge to train ten million AI workers is a tacit admission that access to compute was never going to be the hard part — building the people who can use it well always was. None of it is dishonest, exactly. It's just what happens once the free trial ends.

One More Thing

Buried in Musk's Friday commentary: in the same breath he told users Grok 4.6 was the smart economical choice for today and 4.7 could out-intelligence everything on the market, he admitted a competitor's model is currently sharper than both. Somewhere, a marketing team is trying to figure out how to fit "not our best, but trust us" onto a billboard.
