The Only Slowdown That Actually Happened This Week

Updated September 16, 2026, 22:28 UTC

Everyone spent this week arguing about who should hit the brakes on AI. Wednesday, the only brakes that actually got pulled belonged to the Fed, and it wasn't talking about AI at all.

Editor's Open

Happy Wednesday. Four days ago Sam Altman told Fortune his most advanced model "cannot yet be safely deployed." Today he's reportedly taking early meetings for a funding round that would value OpenAI north of $1.2 trillion. Meanwhile the actual interest-rate hike everyone's been bracing for all week landed right on schedule, and it had nothing to do with any pacing pledge. Grab your coffee: today's about the difference between the slowdown people keep announcing and the one the Fed just delivered.

The Fed Did What No CEO Pledge Could

The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate by 25 basis points Wednesday to a range of 3.75%–4%, its first hike since 2023, with Chair Kevin Warsh telling reporters inflation has been "too high for too long" and needs to move toward the Fed's 2% target "clearly and at sufficient speed." The vote was unanimous, and updated projections showed 16 of 18 committee members expect at least one more hike this year. Markets didn't love it: the Dow shed 631 points (1.21%) to close at 51,461.90, big banks had their worst day since February — JPMorgan fell 1.5%, Goldman Sachs, Wells Fargo, Bank of America, and Citigroup all dropped more than 3% — and the 10-year Treasury yield pushed back above 5%, continuing the exact climb that's been dragging chip stocks lower all week.

Bottom line: after four straight days of essays, pacts, and pledges about pacing AI, the only entity that actually tapped a brake this week was the Fed — and it was aiming at inflation, not intelligence.

OpenAI's Pledge Has an Asterisk Shaped Like $1.2 Trillion

OpenAI is in early talks with investors about a new funding round that would value the company above $1.2 trillion, Bloomberg and the Financial Times reported — with the New York Times separately putting the number closer to $1.5 trillion, a discrepancy nobody's yet reconciled. The talks, reportedly investor-initiated, come as OpenAI eyes a 2027 IPO after Altman said in a Fortune interview last weekend that going public this year would be "ill-advised" given "everything happening with safety," and that OpenAI's most advanced unreleased models "cannot yet be safely deployed." A trillion-plus valuation conversation is a strange thing to be having in the same week.

Bottom line: OpenAI is pacing its public listing and its model releases in public remarks — its private fundraising just keeps accelerating regardless.

China Answers the Slowdown Debate With a Shrug and Another $5 Billion

CNBC reached out to Z.ai, Moonshot, MiniMax, Alibaba, and Tencent this week for comment on the wave of U.S. slowdown warnings; none of them said anything on the record, a striking contrast to Beijing's state media, which spent Tuesday calling the pledge "self-serving" and a "Cold War playbook." One Tencent-backed founder, Ray Von of OpenPie, told CNBC the whole thing is "a little bit oversold" — Chinese labs, he said, don't pay much attention because "that's not the first time" U.S. executives have said this. While the statements stayed quiet, the money didn't: Z.ai settled a $5 billion raise today — a $2 billion Hong Kong share placement plus $3 billion in convertible bonds — just two months after a $4 billion round in July, pushing the company's haul toward $9 billion since summer, earmarked for next-generation model training and inference infrastructure.

Bottom line: Chinese labs aren't debating whether to slow down — they're answering the question with capital, not commentary.

An Open Model Nobody Announced Just Beat GPT and Claude at Browsing the Web

Shanghai AI Lab quietly published Atria Dawn Preview on Hugging Face last week — a 744-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts agentic model, post-trained on Z.ai's GLM-5.2 base, released under an MIT license with no blog post and no pricing page attached. On BrowseComp, a benchmark for actually navigating the live web to answer research questions, it scored 92.5, nudging past GPT-5.6 Sol's 92.2 and clearing Claude Opus 5's 90.8. The accompanying paper, signed by 143 authors, reports results across 16 benchmarks; Atria leads on five of them. It ships with a 256K context window and both standard and FP8-quantized checkpoints, hosted for both international and China-region access.

Bottom line: a free, open-weight model with Chinese roots just out-navigated the flagship paid models from OpenAI and Anthropic on a real-world task — and it showed up with a research paper instead of a launch event.

Quick Hits

  • 🎙️ Google shipped Gemini 3.8 Live and a 3.8 Live Extended Thinking variant, adding background reasoning to its real-time audio model for production-grade voice agents, priced per minute of audio processed.
  • 📱 Apple's Gemini-powered Siri went fully live with iOS 27, with Apple's Private Cloud Compute anonymizing queries before they ever reach Google's servers.
  • 🎓 The University of Leicester rolled out Microsoft 365 Copilot to its entire community of 21,000 students and 4,000 staff, one of the UK's largest single-campus AI deployments to date.
  • 📊 A new Digital Education Council global survey found 88% of students and 77% of faculty now use AI, up 16 points from last year — though 57% of students say they still don't get enough guidance on using it for actual assignments.
  • 🤝 Cohere and Aleph Alpha signed a definitive merger agreement today, becoming a $20 billion, dual-Berlin/Toronto "sovereign AI" challenger — a deal first floated back in April, now official.
  • 🛍️ Sinch's latest consumer survey found just 43% of shoppers expect AI to make holiday shopping easier this year, down from 48% in 2025, with privacy cited as the top concern — enthusiasm cooling even as the tools keep shipping.
The Big Picture

Zoom out and today is the clearest evidence yet that "slow down" has become something companies say, not something they do. OpenAI is talking to investors about a valuation past a trillion dollars days after its CEO called his own top model unsafe to ship. Z.ai answered the entire Western safety debate by raising another $5 billion instead of issuing a statement. Shanghai AI Lab didn't even bother with a press release before quietly beating two flagship Western models on a real benchmark. The only actual deceleration anyone delivered this week came from an institution that has nothing to do with AI at all — a central bank raising rates because inflation, not intelligence, was moving too fast. If you're looking for what a real slowdown looks like, it apparently requires a Federal Reserve, not a pledge.

One More Thing

Buried under today's trillion-dollar funding talk: Luna, the Claude-based AI agent running San Francisco's Andon Market on a three-year, $7,500-a-month lease, just made its first personnel decision — firing a human employee for chronic lateness. The store itself is still reportedly struggling to turn a profit, with visitors describing empty shelves and thin foot traffic. So the AI can't yet keep its own shop solvent, but it can absolutely fire you for showing up late. Progress, of a sort.
