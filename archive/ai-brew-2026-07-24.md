## The Lead Stories

### Washington Says Kimi K3 Has Some "Borrowed" DNA

White House OSTP Director Michael Kratsios publicly accused Chinese AI lab Moonshot of large-scale, covert distillation of Anthropic's Claude Fable model to build its Kimi K3 system — and separately claimed Moonshot obtained export-banned Nvidia GB300 servers by routing access through Thailand to do it. Kratsios said Moonshot built "a sophisticated internal platform to conduct large-scale distillation against U.S. models," cycling through methods of access to dodge detection. The accusation follows Anthropic's own February report tracing more than 3.4 million Claude exchanges to hundreds of fake accounts, some matching the public profiles of senior Moonshot staff. Distillation — training a cheaper model on a pricier one's outputs — isn't automatically illegal, but doing it without permission breaks the labs' terms of service, and smuggled chips break U.S. export law outright. Moonshot hasn't substantively responded.

**Bottom line:** When a government official accuses a company of chip smuggling and copying homework in the same breath, "just distillation" stops being a technical footnote and starts being a trade dispute.

### ChatGPT Plays Doctor Again, Gets Sued the Same Week

OpenAI relaunched Health in ChatGPT for all U.S. users Wednesday, letting anyone on any plan — including the free tier — link Apple Health data and medical records, via a b.well partnership spanning roughly 2.2 million providers, so ChatGPT can discuss their own results in context. It's a rebuild after a January pilot that OpenAI itself called lackluster. The timing is awkward: a day earlier, a Florida man sued OpenAI and Sam Altman, alleging ChatGPT's health advice — delivered in reassuring, faith-inflected language — delayed treatment for a pulmonary embolism and nearly killed him. The suit asks a court to pause the feature's rollout pending an independent safety review. OpenAI says connected health data isn't used for ad targeting or model training, and maintains ChatGPT "is not a doctor."

**Bottom line:** OpenAI is betting people want an AI that knows their medical history — the lawsuit is the reminder that "knows your history" and "gives good advice" aren't the same thing.

### Claude Finally Gets a Grown-Up Voice

Anthropic upgraded Claude's voice mode so it can run on the full Opus and Sonnet models, not just the fast-but-shallow Haiku tier that powered it exclusively until now. That's the trade-off voice assistants usually make — instant replies over careful thought — and Anthropic is letting paying users choose depth over speed for the first time. Practically, it means Claude's voice mode can now handle the kind of multi-step reasoning, coding walk-throughs, or nuanced advice that text-based Claude has always done better than talking-to-your-phone AI. It's a small feature update, but it fits a bigger pattern: labs are racing to make voice interfaces feel less like fast-food ordering and more like actually talking to the smart model.

**Bottom line:** The AI voice assistant that used to feel like a search bar with a microphone is starting to feel like the model you'd actually want handling something that matters.

### India Bets Big on Homegrown Brains

India's IT minister Ashwini Vaishnaw told Parliament this week that the government has greenlit 20 proposals for sovereign, indigenous AI models — 12 large language models and 8 smaller ones — under the IndiaAI Mission, alongside a GPU buildout from today's roughly 54,000 public GPUs to a targeted 100,000 by December. Thirteen additional projects are funded specifically for AI safety work: explainability, bias mitigation, deepfake detection, and privacy-preserving techniques. It's part of a broader pitch to become an AI creator rather than just the world's biggest consumer of someone else's models — a middle path India has been openly weighing as it tries not to end up permanently renting compute from either Washington or Beijing.

**Bottom line:** Every country wants an AI industry it doesn't have to rent — India just put a GPU count and a deadline on that ambition.

## The Big Picture

Strip away the specifics and today's stories are all fighting the same underlying battle: who's earned the trust to be handed more responsibility, more data, or more compute. Washington isn't just annoyed that Kimi K3 is good — it's accusing Moonshot of getting there by breaking rules, which reframes a technical rivalry as a legal one. OpenAI isn't just launching a health feature — it's asking millions of people to hand over medical records to a company being sued over the same category of advice. And India isn't just buying GPUs — it's explicitly trying to avoid needing anyone's permission at all. Compute and capability used to be the whole story; increasingly, the story is whether the people building this stuff have earned the right to be trusted with what they're now asking for.

## One More Thing

The supercomputer Jensen Huang wheeled into the Naval Postgraduate School this week runs on Nvidia's "Grace Blackwell" architecture — named for computing pioneer Grace Hopper and mathematician David Blackwell, neither of whom ever touched a GPU. Somewhere, a Navy officer candidate is now doing coursework on a machine named after two people who did their most famous work with punch cards and chalkboards.
