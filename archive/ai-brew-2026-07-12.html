Apple lawyers up, robots go public, and the Fed hires a VC

The Snapshot
$2.5B — Agility Robotics' SPAC deal, on track to make it the first pure-play humanoid robot maker on a U.S. exchange.
400+ — former Apple employees Apple says are now working at OpenAI, per the trade-secret lawsuit it just filed.
30–46% — the share of U.S. enterprise AI token usage now running on Chinese open models, up from 11% a year ago.
$65M — Ollama's fresh Series B, on the strength of nearly 9 million developers running open models locally.

Mood: it's the kind of Sunday where lawyers, central bankers, and robots all showed up to the same news cycle. Nobody's taking the weekend off.

Happy Sunday. If you were hoping AI would clock out for the weekend, bad news: Apple just accused OpenAI of stealing its secrets employee by employee, the Federal Reserve recruited a venture capitalist to help it think about robots and your job, and three humanoid-robot makers all made moves toward Wall Street in the same week. Grab your coffee — this one's got teeth.

Apple Bites Back at OpenAI

Apple sued OpenAI in federal court in Northern California Friday, alleging a coordinated scheme to steal trade secrets tied to consumer hardware — Apple says the theft happened "at every level," from rank-and-file engineers to OpenAI's own chief hardware officer, Tang Tan, a former Apple VP. The filing claims Tan directed job candidates to bring "actual parts" from Apple to interviews for "show and tell," and separately accuses ex-Apple engineer Chang Liu of downloading dozens of confidential files before joining OpenAI's hardware team. Apple says more than 400 of its former employees now work at OpenAI. The two companies partnered to build ChatGPT into iOS just two years ago — now they're suing each other over the literal parts bin.

Bottom line: The AI industry's talent war just graduated to the courtroom, and Apple made clear it's willing to burn a partnership to protect whatever hardware it's building next.

The Fed Gets a Venture Capitalist

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh named the leaders of five new external task forces this week, and the one studying AI's economic impact goes to a striking trio: Andreessen Horowitz co-founder Marc Andreessen, Stanford economist Charles I. Jones, and Microsoft Xbox CEO Asha Sharma. Their job: figure out how AI reshapes productivity and jobs, then feed that into how the Fed thinks about monetary policy — because if AI is genuinely juicing GDP growth, that changes how aggressively the central bank needs to fight inflation. It's the clearest signal yet that the Fed sees AI less as a tech-sector story and more as a macroeconomic one it can't treat as background noise anymore.

Bottom line: When the people who set interest rates start building AI research units, it's official — AI isn't just changing your job, it's changing how the economy itself gets measured.

China's Open Models Are Eating Everyone's Lunch

A CNBC investigation this week found Chinese AI models now account for 30% to 46% of enterprise API token usage flowing through U.S. developer platforms like OpenRouter — up from 11% over the prior year and just 4.5% in early 2025. DeepSeek alone routes 17.6% of all OpenRouter tokens, with Alibaba's Qwen close behind. The draw is blunt: Chinese open-weight models run 60-90% cheaper than Anthropic's or OpenAI's flagships. The trend crystallized further this month when Z.ai's GLM-5.2 posted 80x customer growth on Vercel's AI Gateway in its first week, with Vercel's own CEO calling himself "genuinely impressed, almost shocked" by its coding chops.

Bottom line: Developers are voting with their API calls, and cost is beating brand — a margin problem for OpenAI and Anthropic, and a bigger headache for Washington's hopes of keeping Chinese AI out of corporate America.

Humanoid Robots Are Ready for Their IPO

Three humanoid-robot makers moved toward public markets within days of each other this week. Agility Robotics signed a $2.5 billion SPAC deal with Churchill Capital Corp XI, backed by Amazon, Nvidia, and SoftBank, aiming to become the first pure-play humanoid company on a U.S. exchange. Unitree's roughly $618 million Shanghai IPO cleared final registration after a record 104-day review, with its G1 robot's price dropping to $13,500. And Tesla began converting the Fremont line that built its last Model S into an Optimus factory, targeting production by late summer. Robots, in other words, are no longer just a lab demo — they're a balance-sheet item.

Bottom line: Investors who missed the chip rally are getting a second entry point, betting the next trillion-dollar AI market walks on two legs instead of living in a data center.

Quick Hits

  • 🕵️ Meta's new AI-image detector verified 100% of original AI-generated images but failed to flag 55% of the same images once cropped, a Reuters analysis found — bad news for spotting deepfakes this election season.
  • 🔌 DeepSeek is developing its own AI inference chip to cut reliance on both Nvidia and Huawei, sources told Reuters — a sign Beijing's chip controls are pushing China toward self-sufficiency instead of stalling it.
  • 🧭 Mistral released Robostral Navigate, an 8B model that steers robots using a single ordinary camera instead of LiDAR, beating multi-camera rivals on the industry's standard navigation benchmark.
  • 🦙 Ollama raised a $65 million Series B led by Theory Ventures, now serving nearly 9 million developers running open models locally.
  • 🏛️ Illinois signed the Artificial Intelligence Safety Measures Act, becoming the first state to require independent third-party safety audits of large AI systems.
  • 📈 Nvidia stock jumped 4% Friday to close at $210.96, as Wall Street kept betting the AI infrastructure buildout has room to run.
The Big Picture

Zoom out and today's stories are really one story: AI has stopped being a product category and started being load-bearing infrastructure for everything else. Apple and OpenAI are suing each other over hardware secrets because the next AI device, not the next chatbot, is the real prize. The Fed is studying AI because it might already be moving GDP and labor numbers enough to change interest-rate math. Developers are picking Chinese models over American ones for the most unglamorous reason there is — price — and it's working well enough to worry Washington. And robots are graduating from lab demos to SPACs and IPOs, which means Wall Street thinks physical AI is investable, not just impressive. None of this is about which chatbot is smartest anymore. It's about who owns the courts, the capital, the cost curve, and the factory floor.

One More Thing

OpenAI says its new GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra model spent under an hour and ran 64 parallel subagents to produce a proof of the Cycle Double Cover Conjecture, a graph-theory problem that's stumped mathematicians since 1973. The proof hasn't been peer-reviewed yet, and the conjecture has fooled brilliant humans with "solved it" moments before — but if it holds up, it's one of the first times a model gets credited as sole author on a genuine open math problem, not just a benchmark score.
