FRIDAY, AUGUST 21, 2026

Everybody's Either Borrowing Big or Taking Back What They Said Yesterday

The Snapshot
$100B — the debt package Broadcom's reportedly assembling with Blackstone and Apollo to keep AI chips flowing to Anthropic and other frontier labs
8,000 — robotaxis Nevada just cleared for Las Vegas streets, split across Tesla, Uber, and Waymo
394,300 — New York's tech workforce, edging out the Bay Area's for the first time in 13 years of the same annual count
44% — Anthropic's share of business AI spending on Ramp's platform, still ahead of OpenAI's 40% — but OpenAI's closing fast
$2.3B — the last valuation of Rebellions, the Korean chip startup Nvidia just started courting
Friday's theme: everybody's either borrowing big, buying in, or quietly walking back something they said yesterday.
Happy Friday. Nvidia spent Thursday denying a story about its own chip roadmap that was making headlines less than a day earlier, Broadcom went looking for a debt pile roughly the size of a small country's GDP to keep building chips nobody's finished paying for, and New York quietly out-teched the Bay Area for the first time since anyone started counting. Grab your coffee — today's about who's actually holding the bag when the financing music stops.

Broadcom's Credit Card Bill Just Hit Nine Zeros

Broadcom is in talks with Blackstone and Apollo to raise as much as $100 billion in debt — roughly $60-70 billion in senior notes plus another $30 billion in junior financing, routed through a special-purpose vehicle — to fund custom AI chips for Anthropic and other frontier labs, extending the $35 billion partnership the three companies announced in June. Anthropic alone is expected to account for more than 40% of the $100-billion-plus in AI-chip revenue Broadcom projects for next year. Structuring the debt off Broadcom's own balance sheet, while the company still guarantees part of the senior tranche, is exactly the kind of arrangement that's made bond traders increasingly twitchy about how much of the AI buildout is real demand versus vendors financing their own customers.

Bottom line: the same circular-financing worry investors flagged about Nvidia's OpenAI guarantee earlier this week just showed up again, this time with an extra zero on the end.

Sin City Rolls the Dice on 8,000 Robotaxis

Nevada's Transportation Authority unanimously approved permits this week letting Tesla, Uber, and Waymo run up to 8,000 robotaxis across Clark County over the next year — Tesla cleared for 5,000, Waymo for 1,000, and Uber for another 1,000-plus through partnerships with Motional and Zoox. The vehicles are capped at 45 mph, largely confined to the Strip corridor, banned from airport runs, and required to display "Robotaxi" markings and warn riders before every trip. Nobody expects all 8,000 on the road soon — Tesla's own Cybercab engineer testified the 5,000 figure "has always been a ceiling," not a target — but it's the largest robotaxi permit batch any U.S. city has approved at once.

Bottom line: Nevada just wrote the biggest permission slip in robotaxi history and immediately admitted nobody's going to use the whole thing — regulators are learning to approve for the hype, not the rollout.

Nvidia Un-Ships the Chip It Was Shipping Yesterday

Less than 24 hours after The Information reported Nvidia planned small-batch shipments of a China-tailored AI chip by year-end — built on Groq-licensed LPU technology, engineered to clear U.S. export rules, with customers reportedly already placing orders — Nvidia flatly denied it Thursday: "We have no LPU sales in the China market today, and no China-specific LPU product in our roadmap." The denial lands five days before Nvidia reports fiscal second-quarter earnings on August 26, a report already carrying outsized expectations, and just as Beijing has spent weeks quietly discouraging Chinese firms from bringing in even the American chips they're cleared to buy.

Bottom line: whether the denial is about the chip not existing or the timing not being right, Nvidia clearly decided "not yet" was the safer thing to say five days before earnings.

New York Steals Silicon Valley's Crown, Sort Of

CBRE's annual Scoring Tech Talent report shows the New York metro area now has 394,300 tech workers versus the Bay Area's 375,730 — the first time in the study's 13-year history that New York leads on raw headcount, driven largely by Bay Area layoffs shrinking its base while New York's finance industry quietly hoovers up AI talent alongside its traditional tech sector. The Bay Area still ranks first overall once CBRE weights in wages, talent concentration, and graduate pipelines, so Wall Street's hiring spree hasn't matched Silicon Valley's density yet. Both markets are growing where it counts, though: AI-skilled tech workers grew 45% nationally over the past year, and San Francisco and New York each added more than 20,000 AI jobs since mid-2025.

Bottom line: New York didn't out-innovate the Bay Area — it just discovered that when finance firms start paying AI-engineer salaries, engineers show up.
Quick Hits
  • 🤝 Nvidia is in early talks with Korean chip startup Rebellions about a partnership, investment, or acquisition, after Jensen Huang met the company's CEO in Santa Clara this week — Rebellions was last valued around $2.3 billion and is prepping its own IPO.
  • 💱 Binance launched "Agent OS," letting AI tools like ChatGPT, Claude Code, and Cursor trade crypto on users' behalf through dedicated subaccounts with no withdrawal access — though there's no cap on what an agent can lose inside its own sandbox.
  • ⚖️ Anthropic is publicly backing a Massachusetts bill that would make it the first U.S. state requiring frontier labs to undergo independent catastrophic-risk reviews every 120 days; OpenAI is fighting it, warning inconsistent state rules will "just cause confusion."
  • 📈 New data from Ramp shows OpenAI closing the gap with Anthropic among U.S. business spenders — Anthropic still leads 44% to 40%, but OpenAI's growing faster this quarter, with GPT-5.6 Sol cited as a developer favorite.
  • 📰 Google rolled out a "favorite source" button letting readers flag publishers they want boosted across Search, Discover, and News — a small olive branch as AI Overviews keep gutting referral traffic, with some publishers reporting drops as steep as 89%.
The Big Picture

Zoom out and today's stories share a theme: confidence outrunning certainty. Broadcom's borrowing nine figures to build chips for a customer base that's still mostly future contracts; Nevada approved 8,000 robotaxi permits that even the biggest permit-holder admits it won't fill; Nvidia spent a day denying a chip plan that sounded entirely plausible the day before. None of that means the optimism is wrong — Anthropic's actual revenue and New York's actual hiring numbers are real, not vaporware. But increasingly the industry's moves are sized for the future everyone's betting on, not the one that's already arrived, and the gap between "we've secured the financing" and "we've shipped the thing" is where all the real risk is quietly sitting.

One More Thing

Linear, the project-tracking tool half of Silicon Valley runs its sprints through, published new data this week showing AI now writes just under half of everything created in the app — two years ago, fewer than one issue in a thousand came from AI. Teams with a coding agent attached now triple their weekly pull requests. The twist: none of that is actually saving anyone time. PR review has become such a bottleneck — review time up 91%, AI-written PRs waiting 4.6x longer to even get looked at — that overall product-development time is going up, not down. Somewhere, a team lead is staring at a burndown chart that refuses to burn down.
