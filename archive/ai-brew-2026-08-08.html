AI Wrote a Virus, Elon Wrote a Check, and Everybody's Building Their Own Everything

Friday's theme: everybody's scaling something — chip capacity, model size, legal exposure — and the rulebook hasn't caught up to any of it.

Happy Friday. Elon Musk broke ground on a $16.8 billion chip factory instead of waiting in Nvidia's line, ByteDance is reportedly training a model three times the size of China's last frontier contender, and Washington started quietly auditing how Chinese firms are renting their way around chip export rules. Then, almost as a footnote, a Stanford-led team announced their AI had designed sixteen actual, working viruses that don't exist in nature — and the safety net meant to catch a bad one wasn't built for that. Grab your coffee: today's about capability outrunning the rules built to contain it, on four different fronts at once.

An AI Wrote a Virus. The Virus Worked.

Stanford and Arc Institute researchers published results in Science on Thursday showing they used Evo 2, a genomic AI model, to design complete viral genomes from scratch — sequences that match nothing found in nature. They synthesized the designs in the lab, and 16 came alive as fully functional bacteriophages (viruses that infect bacteria, not people). It's the first time generative AI has designed a working virus from a blank page rather than tweaking an existing one. A companion editorial from Johns Hopkins biosecurity researchers, published alongside the study, was blunt: the governance needed to safely steer this capability doesn't exist yet. Evo 2 was trained to exclude human-infecting virus data, and no publicly released AI system is known to design pathogens that infect people — but the screening tools meant to flag suspicious DNA orders were built to catch known dangerous sequences, not ones an AI invented an hour ago. No U.S. law currently requires either check.

Bottom line: the models can already write biology that works. The safety net that's supposed to catch a dangerous one is still checking against a list of things we've already seen — which, by definition, an AI-designed genome isn't.

Elon Musk Is Building His Own Chip Factory Because Waiting Wasn't Vibing

Tesla and SpaceX confirmed plans this week for "Terafab," a vertically integrated semiconductor plant in Grimes County, Texas, with $16.8 billion committed just to get phase one running — filings suggest the full multi-phase build could eventually reach $119 billion. The plant is slated to make chips for Optimus robots, Cybercabs, and SpaceX's orbital data centers, with combined projected demand north of 1 terawatt of compute — more than the entire world currently produces. Texas kicked in a $30 million enterprise grant, and the project promises at least 3,000 jobs. It's the same instinct that built Tesla's in-house battery supply chain, aimed now at silicon: if you can't be sure of getting enough chips from someone else, build the fab yourself.

Bottom line: when a rocket company starts pouring concrete for its own chip fab, that's not diversification — it's a bet that the chip shortage isn't ending anytime soon, and owning the factory beats standing in line for one.

Washington Is Auditing How China Keeps Getting Nvidia's Best Chips

Bloomberg reported Friday that the Bureau of Industry and Security is now systematically reviewing how Chinese AI firms access advanced Nvidia chips despite export restrictions — not through smuggling, but by legally renting compute located in other countries. Investigators are building two lists: one of countries serving as physical black-market routes for chips flowing into China, another of countries where Chinese firms remotely tap chips they could never import directly. Remote rental isn't illegal today. The review reportedly followed Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 release last month, which scored close to Anthropic and OpenAI's latest models and raised questions about how a Chinese lab got that kind of compute in the first place.

Bottom line: export controls choke the box at the border, but training happens in the cloud — and if a Chinese firm can rent the chip instead of ship it, the border was never really the bottleneck.

TikTok's Parent Company Is Quietly Building a Monster Model

ByteDance is training a new AI model with roughly 10 trillion parameters — more than three times the size of Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 and reportedly aimed at rivaling Anthropic's Mythos, industry estimates put at around 8 trillion. The model is currently in pretraining, a phase that typically runs three to six months before release, and ByteDance declined to comment. Neither Anthropic nor OpenAI has officially disclosed their latest models' parameter counts, so exact comparisons are rough — but the direction is clear: after a year of the industry insisting efficiency mattered more than raw scale, China's best-funded labs are betting size still wins.

Bottom line: the "smaller, smarter" consensus had a good run. Apparently nobody told ByteDance, which just signed up to out-scale everyone.

The Big Picture

Four stories, one plot: capability is outrunning the infrastructure and rules meant to contain it. Musk isn't waiting for Nvidia's order queue, so he's pouring $16.8 billion into his own fab. ByteDance isn't waiting to see if "smaller is smarter" holds up, so it's training something three times the size of China's last frontier model. Washington isn't waiting for the next smuggling bust, so it's auditing the legal loopholes instead. And in a lab in California, a genomic AI didn't wait for permission either — it just wrote sixteen working viruses, and the screening system built to catch a dangerous one is still checking for sequences somebody's already seen. None of this is a single runaway story. It's the same gap showing up in chips, models, geopolitics, and biology: the tools for building and scaling keep shipping faster than anyone's finished writing the rules for using them safely.

One More Thing

Buried in Friday's news: OpenAI's first hardware product, co-designed with Jony Ive's studio, is reportedly shaped like a donut, sized like a hockey puck, has moving parts "for personality," and will cost north of $300 when it ships sometime in 2027. No screen. Just a battery-powered speaker with cameras and sensors that's supposedly easy to carry around one-handed. Somewhere, a product designer is very proud of a doughnut, and somewhere else, a spreadsheet is quietly wondering what a moving part on a smart speaker is actually for.
