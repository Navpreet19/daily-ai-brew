Friday's theme: everybody's arguing about what to call this era, while the money and the paperwork move on without waiting for an answer.

Happy Friday. OpenAI spent Thursday teasing, leaking, and finally shipping GPT-6 Astra, and company president Greg Brockman used the phrase "AGI era" in front of reporters without quite saying his company built it. A few days earlier, the G20's biggest economies — including China — signed onto a US-drafted pledge to mostly leave AI alone, right as Sacramento sent Governor Newsom a stack of thirty bills doing the opposite. Grab your coffee: today's about who gets to define the moment we're in, and who's quietly writing the actual rules for it anyway.

OpenAI Ships GPT-6 Astra, Everyone Argues About What to Call It

OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra on Thursday, its successor to GPT-5.6 Sol and, the company says, its most capable and best-aligned model yet — able to handle open-ended computer tasks like filling out forms, building spreadsheets, and running multi-step research without much hand-holding. On a press call, Brockman told reporters "welcome to the AGI era," then hedged immediately, calling it "not unreasonable" to think the industry had arrived rather than declaring OpenAI had gotten there itself. The more concrete news: Astra is the first model to cross OpenAI's "Critical" tier for cybersecurity risk under its own Preparedness Framework, scoring 100% on the ExploitBench benchmark unsafeguarded and surfacing two real zero-day vulnerabilities during testing, which OpenAI is now reporting to maintainers. Its most capable cyber features are rolling out first to government and critical-infrastructure partners through OpenAI's Daybreak program, with everyday ChatGPT subscribers following in the coming days.

Bottom line: OpenAI's marketing said "AGI" carefully enough to generate headlines without quite committing to them; its own safety filing said "Critical cybersecurity risk" plainly enough that nobody had to guess what that meant.

The G20 Tells AI Regulators: Not So Fast

All twenty G20 members, including China, signed onto the US-drafted "Carolina Principles" this week at a two-day Innovation Ministerial in Chapel Hill, North Carolina, co-hosted by White House tech adviser Michael Kratsios and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick. The non-binding framework asks countries to reserve new AI-specific regulation for problems existing sector rules genuinely can't handle, steer funding toward foundational research, and skip standing up new dedicated AI oversight bodies. It's a deliberate counterweight to the EU's approach — the same bloc that, days earlier, decided ChatGPT already fit neatly inside an existing rulebook as a "search engine." China's signature is the real surprise: a government that maintains tight state control over its own AI sector just co-signed a US pitch for lighter rules everywhere else.

Bottom line: the world's largest economies just agreed on one thing about AI regulation — that they'd all rather write less of it, at least on paper.

Crusoe Triples Its Price Tag on the Back of One Contract

Data-center developer Crusoe closed a $3 billion-plus funding round at a $30 billion valuation, led by Atreides Management and Valor Equity Partners with Mubadala Capital joining in — triple the roughly $10 billion valuation it carried less than a year ago. The company started in 2018 as a crypto miner burning off stranded natural gas; today it builds and operates AI data centers for customers including Meta, Microsoft, and OpenAI. The catalyst was a $13 billion, five-year cloud deal to supply quant trading firm Jane Street with GPU capacity, which reportedly pulled additional investors into the round once it closed. Crusoe is also now in early, informal talks with Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan, and Bank of America about a potential IPO, though no bank has been formally hired yet.

Bottom line: Crusoe spent seven years mining crypto off flared gas to become, almost by accident, one of AI's most important landlords — and Jane Street just made that bet worth $30 billion.

Sacramento Sends Newsom Thirty Bills, One Signature at a Time

California's legislature closed out its 2026 session by passing roughly thirty AI-related bills, all now sitting on Governor Gavin Newsom's desk with a September 30 deadline to sign or veto. The headliners: SB 947, a revised version of the "No Robo Bosses Act" Newsom vetoed last year, which would bar employers from firing workers based solely on an automated decision and require notice when AI plays a primary role in one; AB 1883, which would ban workplace AI tools from inferring employees' emotional states or collecting their neural data; and SB 1119, "Adam's Law," which would require safety testing and stronger guardrails for companion chatbots that talk with teens, named for a California teenager whose 2025 death was linked to one. It passed the legislature nearly unanimously, and even OpenAI has publicly urged Newsom to sign it.

Bottom line: while Washington spends the week asking the world to regulate AI less, California just spent its session writing down, bill by specific bill, exactly what "regulate it more" looks like in practice.

The Big Picture

Zoom out and today's stories are a tug-of-war over who gets to define this moment. OpenAI wants the story to be that AGI has arrived, even while hedging the actual claim; the G20 wants the story to be that AI needs less oversight, even as its biggest member signs that framework abroad without changing a thing at home. California isn't trying to define the era at all — it's writing down, line by line, what happens when an algorithm fires you or a chatbot talks to your teenager, regardless of what label anyone else settles on. And underneath all of it, the actual infrastructure keeps compounding no matter who wins the argument: Crusoe tripled its valuation on one contract, and Anthropic's revenue more than doubled in a quarter it hasn't even finished pitching to Wall Street yet. The vocabulary fight is still unsettled. The money already voted.

One More Thing

Buried under today's valuations and vocabulary fights: Google Research, HHMI's Janelia campus, and collaborators in the UK published, in the journal Cell, the first complete wiring diagram of a male fruit fly's entire central nervous system — 166,000 neurons and 125 million synaptic connections, the largest brain map ever assembled. None of it would have been possible without AI: machine-learning models did the heavy lifting of tracing and segmenting each neuron out of raw microscope imagery, a task that would have taken human researchers decades by hand. Somewhere between the AGI debates and the funding rounds, AI quietly finished mapping an entire nervous system this week. Not a bad way to spend a Thursday.
