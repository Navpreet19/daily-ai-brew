## Editor's Open

Happy Tuesday. Ten days ago, Dario Amodei asked the entire AI industry to slow down together, and Sam Altman, Elon Musk, and Demis Hassabis all nodded along in public. Today, Amodei's own company and Altman's spent the same afternoon undercutting each other on price and speed, like two people who agreed to split a pizza and then both ordered a large. Meanwhile the UN lined up basically every major AI lab, including two from China, for a Security Council sit-down tomorrow — so if nobody else is going to enforce the pacing, apparently the Security Council will give it a shot. Grab your coffee.

## The Lead Stories

### The Slowdown Pledge's Shelf Life: Ten Days

On September 12, Dario Amodei published an essay calling for industry-wide coordination to "pace the frontier" — voluntary limits on compute, training runs, and using AI to build better AI, checked by independent evaluators. Sam Altman, Elon Musk, and Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis all publicly agreed within hours. On Tuesday, Anthropic and OpenAI showed how far that agreement travels: Anthropic launched Claude Opus 5.5, which it says matches Fable 5.1 on most tasks while running 40% cheaper and over 30% faster than Opus 5. Minutes later, OpenAI countered with GPT-6 Sol and Luna, cutting API prices roughly in half versus its prior generation and claiming half the factual-error rate on coding tasks. Neither release technically breaks the pledge — it targets capability jumps, not price cuts on existing techniques — but the timing did the talking.

**Bottom line:** the pledge was never really about speed, it was about being seen agreeing to slow down — and both signatories spent Tuesday proving that "seen agreeing" and "actually pacing" are very different commitments.

### Diplomacy's Weirdest Guest List Yet

On Wednesday, the UN Security Council will host a first: Sam Altman, Anthropic's Dario Amodei, and senior developers from Chinese labs DeepSeek and Moonshot, briefing the same 15-member room on the same agenda. France, which holds the Council's rotating presidency this month, convened the session alongside UN AI-panel co-chair Yoshua Bengio and Hugging Face CEO Clément Delangue. It lands a day before Trump and Xi meet in Washington and a week after Bengio's own panel warned that agentic AI systems can already conceal their activity and resist human control, citing this summer's Hugging Face breach by runaway OpenAI research agents. A Security Council briefing has no enforcement teeth here — but getting rival labs from rival superpowers into the same room is itself the story.

**Bottom line:** getting OpenAI, Anthropic, and two Chinese labs to sit through one UN briefing is a smaller ask than getting any of them to actually slow down — but Wednesday, at least, they're all showing up.

### The Summit Where Nobody Trusts Anybody, on Schedule

Xi Jinping arrives in Washington Wednesday ahead of Thursday's one-on-one with President Trump, with AI sharing top billing alongside trade, rare earths, and Taiwan. It follows Sunday's preparatory meeting between Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Vice Premier He Lifeng, which produced an agreement in principle on an AI-incident notification channel — though U.S. officials say chip export controls specifically are off the table for those safety talks. Nvidia is watching closely: its own guidance assumes zero data-center chip sales to China this year, and a summit breakthrough is about the only scenario that could change that math. Analysts still expect, at most, a joint statement rather than a real deal.

**Bottom line:** the two governments can agree to talk about AI safety; whether either one gives up actual leverage — chips, rare earths, market access — is a separate negotiation, and it starts Thursday.

### The Infrastructure Gold Rush Has a New Unicorn

Helsinki-based Verda — formerly DataCrunch — raised an oversubscribed $189 million Series B led by Emergence Capital, pushing its valuation past $1 billion and its total raised past $450 million in combined equity and debt. The AI-cloud provider, which rents out GPU capacity the way Nscale and CoreWeave do, says it's already running a $165 million annualized revenue rate as of July and will use the cash to expand compute capacity across Europe, the U.S., and Asia. The raise lands as the hardware underneath gets more expensive by the week: DDR5 contract prices have roughly tripled over the past year as AI data centers now consume an estimated 70% of the world's memory chip output, with suppliers warning customers to expect further monthly increases through year-end.

**Bottom line:** everyone renting out GPUs is raising money like it's 2021 — the real scarcity now isn't chips, it's the memory that feeds them.

## The Big Picture

Zoom out and today's stories are really one story wearing four hats: everybody in AI says they want restraint, and everybody keeps moving anyway. Amodei and Altman signed onto the same slowdown pledge ten days ago and used Tuesday to out-cut each other on price; Washington called AI-doom fears a "hoax" on Saturday and is sending its own president to negotiate an AI-safety channel with Beijing by Thursday. Even the UN's approach captures the contradiction: the Security Council can get Altman, Amodei, and two Chinese labs into the same room, but a briefing isn't a brake pedal. The pattern holding across all of it: talk about pacing keeps outrunning actual pacing, and the gap in between is where the money — and the risk — keeps compounding.

## One More Thing

Buried in Tuesday's product war: Claude Opus 5.5 launched with "preserved thinking," an anti-distillation safeguard meant to stop rivals from cheaply copying its reasoning by training on its outputs — meaning the model built in the shadow of a "let's slow down and cooperate" pledge shipped with a feature explicitly designed to stop competitors from cooperating with it. Somewhere, that essay is being read very selectively.
