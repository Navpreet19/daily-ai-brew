Editor's Open

Happy Wednesday. An OpenAI model spent millions of dollars in compute solving a 165-year-old fluid-dynamics problem nobody asked it to solve, Meta shipped the personal AI agent it teased last week, and US intelligence agencies published a receipts-heavy accusation that six Chinese labs have been quietly siphoning off American models for two years. Grab your coffee: today's about capability showing up faster than anyone's ready to supervise it — in a proof, in an agent, and in places nobody was watching closely enough.

The Homework Nobody Assigned, Graded By Nobody Yet

OpenAI said this week that an internal model — "significantly more capable" than GPT-6 Astra and not yet released — produced a proof that three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations can develop a singularity in finite time, resolving one of the six remaining Millennium Prize Problems the Clay Mathematics Institute has offered $1 million for since 2000. The company says the effort took roughly 10,000 agents working in parallel, cost millions of dollars in compute, and has been formally verified in the proof-checking language Lean. OpenAI says it won't claim the prize money, framing the result instead as a capability demonstration. But the announcement landed alongside a separate controversy, reported by Axios, over whether the underlying mathematical insight leaned on unpublished work from outside researchers who never consented to having it used this way — a credit dispute that's now the more-discussed half of the story.

Bottom line: a machine-checked proof is either the biggest applied-math story of the year or a preview of the ugliest authorship fight in it, and right now nobody's fully sorted out which.

Six Labs, One Advisory, Zero Ambiguity

The NSA, CISA, and FBI issued a joint advisory this week naming DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, Alibaba, MiniMax, StepFun, and Z.AI as running "industrial-scale" knowledge-distillation campaigns against US frontier models — including Claude, GPT, Gemini, and Grok — that the agencies say date back to at least late 2024 and route through APIs, cloud platforms, and third-party aggregators to obscure their origin. The advisory goes further than prior distillation chatter by calling the practice "the core — not merely a supplement" of these companies' AI strategy, and specifically notes that DeepSeek's widely cited $5.6 million R1 training cost excludes whatever it spent acquiring the distilled data in the first place. The timing isn't subtle: it lands days before a planned US-China AI summit, and the same week an investigation found Aivres — the renamed US subsidiary of blacklisted Chinese server maker Inspur — had shipped $5.6 billion in Nvidia-powered servers to Southeast Asia since 2024, some of which reportedly ended up serving ByteDance and Alibaba.

Bottom line: Washington keeps trying to build a wall around its best models one export rule at a time, and keeps discovering the wall has a door in it that somebody already walked through.

Meta's Agent Shows Up, Right on Schedule

Meta officially launched Muse this week, the personal AI agent it had been teasing under the "Hatch" codename — a system built to actually do tasks (scheduling, shopping, form-filling, planning) across a user's calendar, email, and apps rather than just answer questions, running inside what Meta calls a dedicated "Muse Secure VM." It's live on the web, iOS, Android, and inside WhatsApp for users 18 and up, free to start with $20 and $100 monthly tiers for heavier use. The launch comes just 13 days after Meta's $18 billion settlement in an unrelated matter, and the same week a watchdog investigation found the company had approved and run hundreds of ads containing suspected AI-generated child sexual abuse material on its platforms between last November and this August — a report Meta hasn't substantively disputed, and one that's already prompted separate US and Australian inquiries.

Bottom line: Meta shipped the agent it promised on time, which is the easy part; convincing anyone that the same trust-and-safety pipeline can be trusted to watch what it's doing is going to be the much longer project.

The Legal Industry's Favorite Startup Gets Richer Again

Legal AI startup Harvey raised $550 million at a $15.5 billion valuation, Bloomberg reported, co-led by new investors Diffusion and Lightspeed Venture Partners — nearly doubling its valuation in about nine months and pushing total funding past $1.55 billion. The company says annual recurring revenue has crossed $400 million, up from a customer base that's more than tripled since March, and now counts 80% of the top 100 law firms, a fifth of the Fortune 500, and half of the Fortune 10 as clients. The round follows Harvey's release of its first post-trained open-weight model and a new benchmarking product, Harvey LAB, aimed at measuring how well legal AI agents actually perform against real casework rather than generic tests.

Bottom line: law was supposed to be one of the professions AI would take years to crack; instead it's turned into one of the clearest proof points that vertical AI, sold to people who bill by the hour, might be the easiest business model in the industry.

The Big Picture

Zoom out and today's stories all trace back to the same gap: capability keeps arriving faster than the systems meant to supervise it. OpenAI's proof, if it holds up, is a genuine leap in what these models can do unsupervised — and the argument over whose unpublished math got fed into it shows nobody built the consent process for that leap in advance. The NSA advisory and the Aivres story are two versions of the same problem at the national level: export controls and distillation defenses written for how chips and models moved a few years ago, colliding with companies that found the gaps months or years before regulators caught up. And Meta launching a genuinely capable personal agent in the same week a watchdog catches its ad system approving AI-generated abuse material isn't really a coincidence — it's what happens when the same company is racing to ship autonomy and still hasn't finished policing the much older, much simpler problem of what runs in its ad slots. The pattern isn't that AI got dangerous overnight. It's that the boring supervisory work — verification, export enforcement, ad review — keeps losing the race to whatever ships next.

One More Thing

Buried under today's proofs and advisories: Inception's new Mercury 2.5 can now generate text faster than most people can read it — 1,100-plus tokens a second, well north of the roughly 200-300 words a minute an average adult reads. Diffusion models work by sketching a rough draft of the whole answer at once and sharpening it in parallel, rather than writing one word at a time like everything else you've used. It's a neat trick right up until you remember the bottleneck was never really the model's typing speed — it's you.
