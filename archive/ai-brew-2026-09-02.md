## Editor's Open

Happy Wednesday. Anthropic signed off on yet another multi-billion-dollar compute deal — its fourth in a matter of months — while across the Atlantic, Brussels quietly reclassified ChatGPT as a search engine, the kind of label that comes with real fines attached. Meanwhile, an Amsterdam startup most people outside enterprise software have never heard of just doubled its valuation in half the time it took Clay to do the same thing two days ago. Grab your coffee — today's about how fast the checks are getting signed on both sides of the AI industry's ledger, and the one place that just decided to start checking them.

## The Lead Stories

### Anthropic Orders a Fourth Helping of Compute

Anthropic has reportedly agreed to rent roughly $35 billion in Nvidia-powered computing capacity from cloud provider Lambda, according to Bloomberg and The Information, with Nvidia chips installed at a Lambda-operated data center and Nvidia — an investor in and supplier to Lambda — reportedly holding the lease on the facility itself. Neither company has confirmed the deal publicly. It's the latest in a run of megadeals Anthropic has struck since spring: roughly $45 billion for capacity at Nscale's West Virginia campus, $50 billion with neocloud Fluidstack, and $45 billion with SpaceX, all to keep Claude's training and inference pipelines fed.

**Bottom line:** when one company's cloud shopping list starts running past $150 billion in a matter of months, "compute shortage" stops being a talking point and starts being the entire business model.

### Brussels Tells ChatGPT: You're a Search Engine Now

The European Commission designated OpenAI's ChatGPT a "Very Large Online Search Engine" under the EU's Digital Services Act on September 1 — the first time regulators have placed a generative AI chatbot in that category, alongside Reddit and Roblox's parallel designation as Very Large Online Platforms. The trigger was scale: ChatGPT's live-search feature reported roughly 159 million average monthly users across the EU, more than triple the 45-million threshold that forces the label. The Commission's reasoning is straightforward — a chatbot that pulls real-time results off the web functions like a search engine, hybrid interface or not. OpenAI now has four months to meet the DSA's toughest obligations, including systemic-risk assessments and independent audits, or face fines of up to 6% of global annual revenue.

**Bottom line:** the EU didn't write a new law to regulate AI — it just decided ChatGPT already fits an old box, and old boxes come with fines that are very much still enforceable.

### Anthropic Ships a Cheaper, Sharper Claude

Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.1 on September 1, alongside a more heavily-safeguarded sibling, Mythos 5.1, restricted to trusted cybersecurity and life-sciences partners — the same underlying model with different guardrails. The company pitches Fable 5.1 as its best yet for coding, knowledge work, and long-running tasks, tuned to fix the root cause of bugs rather than take shortcuts that just make code look finished. Sticker pricing holds at $10 per million input tokens and $50 per million output tokens, matching Fable 5, but Anthropic says most customers will see real costs drop about 25%. The bigger cut is in the fine print: a cached-token read now costs $0.25 per million tokens, down from $1.00 — a 75% reduction, and a far steeper cache discount than Anthropic gives any other Claude model.

**Bottom line:** everyone else in this race is chasing capability; Anthropic's actual pitch this round is that leaving Claude running just got a lot cheaper.

### Wonderful Doubles Up, Twice as Fast as Clay

Enterprise AI startup Wonderful closed a $550 million Series C at a $5 billion valuation, led by Insight Partners with Salesforce joining as a new backer, the Amsterdam- and Israel-based company said Tuesday. That's more than double the $2 billion valuation it carried just six months ago, at its March Series B. Wonderful pitches itself as an "AI operating system" — a model-agnostic layer that coordinates agents, workflows, and integrations across an enterprise, running on any cloud or on-prem — and says it's grown to roughly 650 employees across more than 35 markets since that last round. The new money is earmarked for product development and expanding its deployment teams as enterprise demand for agent orchestration keeps climbing.

**Bottom line:** Clay took fourteen months to double its valuation twice over; Wonderful just did it once in six — proof that in enterprise AI right now, the fastest-growing line item isn't revenue, it's the multiple investors put on the promise of it.

## The Big Picture

Zoom out and today's stories are really one story told four ways: capital in AI is moving so fast that confirmation is starting to lag behind the deal itself. Anthropic's $35 billion Lambda arrangement isn't officially confirmed by either company, and neither is Nvidia's rumored Hugging Face purchase — yet both get treated as functionally real because the dollar figures involved make the specifics almost beside the point. Wonderful didn't get five times more valuable because its revenue quintupled; it got there because investors are pricing in what enterprise AI orchestration could eventually be worth, the same bet that's taken Clay from $3 billion to $7 billion in about a year. The one genuine brake in today's lineup is Brussels, which didn't pass a new AI law to catch up — it just pointed at an old one and said ChatGPT already qualifies. That's probably the more durable pattern going forward: regulators won't out-spend or out-innovate the money, but they can decide which existing rulebook a runaway product falls into, and that alone comes with teeth.

## One More Thing

Buried under today's bigger numbers: a British Heart Foundation-funded AI tool, developed with Imperial College London, can now pull heart-failure and valve-disease signals out of a routine ECG — a test that's never been able to show either condition on its own. In a 67,000-patient study, it correctly flagged up to 81% of heart failure cases and 90% of valve disease, using a test so cheap and common that roughly a billion of them are performed worldwide every year. No new hardware, no new scan — just a machine learning model finding patterns in a century-old squiggly line that cardiologists have been staring at for decades without seeing them. Sometimes the most useful AI story of the day isn't the one with a dollar sign in front of it.
