## The Lead Stories

### TSMC Prints Its Best Quarter Ever

TSMC's full Q2 earnings land today, but the headline number is already locked in: revenue hit a record NT$1.27 trillion (about $39.5 billion), up 36% from a year ago and above the top of the company's own guidance. Analysts expect profit to follow suit, projecting a roughly 55-59% jump in per-share earnings versus last year. The engine is the same one that's been running hot all year — AI chip orders from Nvidia and Apple have TSMC's 3-nanometer production effectively sold out through the end of 2026, and the company is plowing a record ~$56 billion into capex to keep up, on top of a $165 billion commitment to its Arizona campus. The thing to watch on today's call: CoWoS advanced packaging, now a tighter bottleneck than raw wafer capacity.

**Bottom line:** AI's spending ceiling keeps getting redrawn upward — the constraint everyone worried about (chip supply) has been replaced by a narrower one (how fast you can package the chips together).

### Nvidia Cuts Its Own Guest List in Half

Nvidia has quietly slashed the number of Asian companies authorized to buy its AI chips, according to the Financial Times, introducing a stricter "white list" system that more than half of previously approved buyers failed to make. The new checks are hands-on: Nvidia staff are visiting data centers, reviewing contracts, and interviewing end users to confirm where chips actually end up, with extra scrutiny in Singapore, Malaysia, and Japan — server and trading hubs that keep showing up in diversion cases. The crackdown follows U.S. prosecutors charging a Supermicro co-founder and two employees in March over an alleged scheme that routed roughly $2.5 billion of Nvidia chips from Taiwan through Southeast Asia into China. Rejected buyers can reapply once they clean up their compliance.

**Bottom line:** Washington didn't have to write a new law — Nvidia just became its own export-control enforcer, because getting caught once is more expensive than losing a customer.

### Meta's Layoffs Go on Trial — Literally

Twenty-six Meta employees filed a federal lawsuit asking a California court to halt their terminations, scheduled for July 22 as part of an 8,000-person layoff, arguing the company built its termination list using AI. The complaint says Meta leaned on its Metamate assistant, employee "second-brain" agents, AI-token usage dashboards, and algorithmic performance rankings to score workers — and that the metrics quietly punished people who'd taken protected medical, disability, or family leave, since time away from a keyboard reads as low productivity to an algorithm that can't tell the difference. It's reportedly the first U.S. lawsuit directly challenging a major company's use of AI to decide who gets laid off. Meta's response was blunt: "workforce decisions were and are made by people, not AI."

**Bottom line:** Whether or not Meta's algorithm actually decided anything, this lawsuit previews the next decade of labor law — proving what a black box weighed before it fired you.

### China Finally Lets Apple Intelligence In

After roughly two years in regulatory limbo, China's Cyberspace Administration approved Apple Intelligence for iPhones, iPads, Macs, and Vision Pro sold in the country — on the condition that it runs on Alibaba's Qwen model instead of Apple's own, since foreign AI models can't operate unsupervised for Chinese consumers. Once it rolls out (no date yet), users get Qwen's text and image understanding and content generation built directly into Apple's operating systems, no app-switching required. It's a notable reversal from Beijing, which had left Apple Intelligence out of the one major market where the company's phone sales have been sliding fastest — and a coup for Alibaba, whose model just became the default brain inside a few hundred million Chinese iPhones.

**Bottom line:** Apple didn't get to bring its own AI into China — it got permission to rent someone else's, which tells you exactly who has leverage in that relationship.

## The Big Picture

Zoom out and today is really one story told four ways: an enormous, undiminished flow of capital into AI's physical layer — chips, defense drones, generative-video infrastructure, sovereign chip ecosystems — running alongside a growing insistence that somebody prove they're using all of it responsibly. TSMC's record quarter and Helsing's record raise show investors aren't remotely worried about a slowdown. But Nvidia policing its own buyer list, Meta answering for its algorithm in court, and Beijing making Apple wait two years before it would allow foreign AI anywhere near a Chinese iPhone all say the same thing: the buildout was never going to be the hard part. Trust is.

---

## One More Thing

Quick trivia for the group chat: the biggest bottleneck in AI chip production right now isn't the chip. It's CoWoS — Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate, the advanced packaging process that binds compute dies to memory — and TSMC's capacity for it is reportedly booked solid enough that the packaging queue, not the fabs, decides how fast the next generation of AI data centers gets built. Somewhere, an assembly line just became more famous than the silicon it assembles.
