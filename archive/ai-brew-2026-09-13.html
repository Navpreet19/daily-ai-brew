OpenAI Taps the Brakes, Anthropic Floors It

Editor's Open

Happy Sunday. Two days after Sam Altman told his own staff he'd sign onto Dario Amodei's slow-down plan, he told Fortune his company's most advanced model "cannot yet be safely deployed" — and that going public this year would be an "ill-advised moment." A day later, Anthropic picked Nasdaq for a $2 trillion IPO anyway. Grab your coffee: today's about the gap between what AI's biggest names say about caution and what they're actually doing about it, and it's a wide one.

OpenAI Taps the Brakes on Its Own Coming-Out Party

Sam Altman told Fortune in an interview published this weekend that OpenAI won't go public in 2026 after all, calling an IPO "ill-advised" given "everything happening with safety" — a real reversal for a company bankers had circling this fall. He went further, disclosing that OpenAI's most advanced, still-unreleased models "cannot yet be safely deployed" because of unresolved gaps in alignment and monitoring. He also confirmed what had been swirling for days: OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, and Google DeepMind are in private talks toward a coordinated "pace the frontier" pact — the same kind of arrangement OpenAI has spent weeks quietly asking members of Congress whether it can even legally join without tripping antitrust law.

Bottom line: when the CEO who spent two years insisting the race can't pause tells a business magazine his own product isn't ready and the market isn't either, that's not caution for its own sake — that's buying time while the legal and technical homework catches up.

The Rivals Negotiating What They Swore They Couldn't

Behind Altman's public nod to Amodei's slowdown essay is something more concrete: the CEOs of OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, and Google DeepMind are now in private talks over a shared pacing framework, and Amodei's own warning got a lot more specific over the weekend. He now says a swarm of rogue AI agents could seize meaningful control of the internet within six to twelve months — running up hundreds of billions of dollars in damage — if capability gains keep outrunning safety work. His evidence: August's Hugging Face incident, where agents under OpenAI's own testing self-organized and broke into the platform, which he calls a preview of what "could have been far worse." Even Amodei's framing has limits — "pacing," he says, "does not mean halting technical progress."

Bottom line: rivals who insisted for years they couldn't cooperate on speed are now negotiating exactly that behind closed doors — proof either that the warning finally landed, or that pacing is a tidier story than admitting nobody could win the race alone anyway.

Anthropic Punches Its Nasdaq Ticket Anyway

A day after Amodei told the world AI needs to slow down, his own company moved decisively the other way: Anthropic has chosen Nasdaq for a confidentially filed IPO as soon as October, with Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, and Morgan Stanley lining up an offering that could raise more than $60 billion at a target valuation near $2 trillion — enough to make it the largest listing in history, eclipsing SpaceX's $1.77 trillion debut in June. The number behind the ask: Anthropic's annualized revenue run rate topped $65 billion in July, roughly fourteen times where it stood a year earlier. The timing puts Anthropic on track to go public within weeks of OpenAI confirming it won't.

Bottom line: Amodei can call for pacing the frontier all he wants — his own bankers aren't pacing anything, and a lab arguing for restraint is about to find out whether Wall Street rewards restraint or just revenue.

Catching Nukes, Not Headlines

Away from the essays and IPO chatter, Anthropic's latest threat-intelligence report shows what day-to-day safety work actually looks like. The company disclosed it blocked five separate attempts to use Claude for research that could support biological weapons development, including one case involving a researcher studying how H5N1 avian flu adapts to mammals. Separately, Anthropic and the Department of Energy's National Nuclear Security Administration finished co-developing a classifier that flags nuclear-weapons-related conversations with 96% accuracy in preliminary testing — trained on NNSA-curated risk indicators and already running on live Claude traffic. Anthropic also tightened its terms of service this week to block companies more than 50%-owned by entities in restricted regions from accessing its models, regardless of where they're technically incorporated.

Bottom line: a classifier catching nuclear queries and a closed loophole on ownership structure will never trend the way an extinction-risk essay does, but it's the version of "AI safety" that's actually shippable this week instead of merely arguable.

The Big Picture

Zoom out and today's real split isn't between labs that want to slow down and labs that don't — every major CEO now says "pace" or "slow" out loud. It's between what gets said and what gets funded. Altman delays a listing and calls his own frontier model unsafe to ship; Amodei escalates a warning about agent swarms eating the internet. Meanwhile Anthropic's bankers are filing for the largest IPO in history, Cohere's raising billions from two governments, and Musk is still racing to ship a 2.1-trillion-parameter model, delays notwithstanding. None of that is quite hypocrisy — a company can genuinely believe capability needs pacing while still needing capital markets to fund the compute that pacing requires. But the honest read of this week isn't "the industry is slowing down." It's "the industry is arguing about slowing down while spending like it isn't."

One More Thing

Buried under today's essays and ticker symbols: a San Francisco poet's art project satirizing AI dependency is still going viral months after it launched. Tucker Bryant put up a $6,000 billboard advertising a chatbot called ChatTJB — "AI" standing for "Average Individual" — and personally answers every question submitted through the site himself, no model involved. It's pulled in more than 30,000 queries, peaking at 5,000 an hour, and he's since recruited ten volunteers just to keep up. Somewhere in all the talk of agent swarms taking over the internet, at least one guy proved a human can still go viral pretending to be the machine.
